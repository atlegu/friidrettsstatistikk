# Discussion

## 4.1 Summary of findings

From this 14-year follow-up of 2,123 young athletes, four findings emerge. First, competition volume at ages 13–14 was strongly and prospectively associated with retaining senior competition. Second, the association replicated across both birth cohorts and withstood structural controls (region, birth quarter, club size). Third, the dropout signal decomposed into two independent components: early behavioral heterogeneity already visible at ages 13–14, and a within-athlete pull-back in the year preceding departure (Section 3.5). Fourth, higher event-category concentration within track and field was associated with higher retention — a different construct from, not a contradiction of, multisport diversification (§4.6). Cox time-to-cessation analyses reinforce these findings: among athletes still active at age 16, milestone-window volume remained strongly associated with subsequent cessation (HR = 0.60 per SD; HR = 0.14 in years 0–3).

## 4.2 Behavior explains more variance in retention than performance

Behavior (competitions entered) explained more variance in senior retention than performance (Tyrving score) even when both were measured in the same window (Section 3.6; Supplementary Table S15): sex + Tyrving yielded AUC = 0.607, sex + pre-milestone volume 0.740, and adding Tyrving to volume added essentially nothing (0.737). Our reading is that performance reflects capability, while volume reflects the athlete's own choices about how much to take part — a reading supported by the open-entry structure of Norwegian youth athletics, in which athletes decide what to enter and clubs cover entry fees (Section 2.2) — and that these choices matter more for disengagement than capability does.

Two qualifications follow. There is an epistemic asymmetry: predictor and outcome both index registered competitive activity, so "behavior outperforms performance" describes the temporal structure through which disengagement becomes observable, not a causal mechanism; the results are consistent with an engagement-balance account but do not establish it. And low or declining volume can have causes unrelated to waning engagement. Cost is one: survey evidence shows cost-related reasons for leaving organized sport are cited more often by Norwegian youth from lower-income families (Espedalen & Seippel, 2024). In the setting studied here this channel is attenuated — clubs ordinarily pay entry fees and travel distances are typically short (Section 2.2) — but not eliminated (membership fees, equipment, occasional longer travel), and the register holds no socioeconomic information, so resource-constrained and motivationally driven pull-back cannot be fully separated. Deliberate periodization — competing less in order to peak later — is another, but it is unlikely to explain the pattern: the decline unfolds across successive calendar years from age 14 onward rather than within a season, and it is followed by exit from the register rather than by a performance peak. The moderate volume–performance correlation and the within-stratum volume effects (Section 3.6) likewise argue against reading volume as a training-strategy artifact.

## 4.3 There exists a pull-back effect — and also an early heterogeneity effect

Is the main effect a genuine within-athlete pull-back from a common starting point, or early differentiation between athletes on different trajectories? Descriptively, future retainers and dropouts already differed at ages 13–14 (median 13/17 vs. 8/8 meets), with the greatest divergence at ages 14–15 (Figure 2, Table 2). The level-and-change analysis (Section 3.5) shows that both effects exist: volume level at age 14 and change from age 14 to 15 each carried significant independent information, and model fit improved substantially when they were combined. Part of the dropout signal is thus early behavioral heterogeneity — consistent with retrospective evidence that future dropouts already differed from persisting peers in their developmental activities years before leaving (Wall & Côté, 2007) — while an equally substantial part is a within-athlete pull-back among the not-yet-dropped-out in the year preceding formal departure.

## 4.4 Consistent with process accounts of withdrawal

Since we assess behavior rather than motivational processes, we do not claim empirical support for any single framework; but the observed footprint is consistent with the main process accounts. In the Sport Commitment Model (Scanlan et al., 1993, 2016), the desire and resolve to continue are shaped by enjoyment, personal investments, involvement opportunities, attractive alternatives, and social constraints; the current model distinguishes *enthusiastic* commitment — a wholehearted "want to" — from *constrained* commitment, a more externally driven sense of obligation that is less sustainable and has been linked to burnout. Declining competition volume years before exit is consistent with an eroding commitment balance, although the register cannot distinguish whether waning enthusiasm or accumulating constraint drives the decline.

Qualitative withdrawal-as-process accounts (Ebaugh, 1988; Eliasson & Johansson, 2021) describe dropout as a gradual deliberative passage — first doubts, weighing of alternatives, a turning point — among athletes who remain formally involved: precisely what the register shows at age 15, when a typical future dropout was still competing in approximately three meets despite eventually stopping altogether. In Ebaugh's terms, the register cannot observe first doubts or the weighing of alternatives, but it can observe what that deliberation looks like behaviorally: two to three seasons of thinning participation preceding the final exit. The lead time we measure is the observable footprint of the process Eliasson and Johansson (2021) reconstructed from interviews — and its length matches their description of withdrawal processes that can span years. Espedalen's (2025) picture of a small, deeply committed minority alongside a larger group participating for casual enjoyment is likewise compatible, noting that these are distinct dimensions: constrained commitment is not equivalent to casual participation, and can occur even among highly invested athletes (Scanlan et al., 2016). The register cannot adjudicate between these mechanisms, but it demonstrates that *some* mechanism left a footprint large enough to be observable in registered activity.

## 4.5 Sex effect

Kaplan–Meier curves showed no clear sex difference in retention (log-rank *p* = .33), contrasting with survey-based reports of higher female dropout in Norwegian youth sport (Bakken, 2019). The multivariable hazard ratio for females was significant in Cohort A (HR = 1.19, *p* = .006) but not in Cohort B (HR = 1.02, *p* = .83). Critically, the behavioral mechanism was similar across sexes (Supplementary Table S7): milestone-window volume reduced dropout hazard similarly for males (HR = 0.42) and females (HR = 0.46), with C-indices of 0.843 for each — so any residual sex effect operates *above*, not *through*, the behavioral pathway.

## 4.6 Within-sport event concentration is a different construct from multisport diversification

Event-category concentration at ages 13–14 predicted *greater* senior retention (OR = 1.35 per SD of HHI; 95% CI [1.16, 1.57]), consistently across cohorts (OR = 1.24 and 1.58) and in the Cox model (HR = 0.80). It is tempting to read this against the diversification literature, but that literature primarily concerns diversification *across sports* — multisport sampling versus early single-sport specialization and its consequences for later elite performance and athlete health (Côté & Hancock, 2016; Güllich et al., 2022). Our HHI instead measures concentration across event categories *within* a single sport, conditional on already participating in that sport, and the register contains no information about involvement in other sports: an athlete with concentrated track and field participation may simultaneously be a multisport sampler — entering fewer event categories could reflect time devoted to handball or football rather than narrow athletic focus. The two constructs are therefore related but not interchangeable. Within-sport and sport-specific evidence is itself mixed: markers of early specialization predicted burnout and dropout in youth swimming (Larson et al., 2019), while others have argued that blanket condemnation of early specialization outruns the available evidence (Baker et al., 2021). The outcome also differs — retention at age 20, not elite performance — and our estimate is conditional on entry into the competitive funnel, a logically later question than the one the sampling literature asks. Finally, HHI is not a proxy for main-event performance: HHI and primary-category Tyrving were essentially uncorrelated (r = −0.05), including primary-category performance left the HHI coefficient unchanged (OR = 1.32 vs. 1.36), and primary-category Tyrving did not itself predict dropout (*p* > .12). Governing bodies should read this result neither as a license to push early specialization nor as grounds for forced diversification among motivated adolescents who intend to continue competing.

## 4.7 What registers bring

Registers address two structural problems in dropout research: withdrawn athletes are systematically hard to recruit into surveys, and retrospective accounts compress a continuous process into a remembered decision. Registration records instead provide a complete population and continuous, rather than reconstructed, behavioral sequences. Registers bring no new theory; they bring a longitudinal behavioral test of theories built largely on cross-sectional or short-prospective evidence — here a baseline-only signal with AUC = 0.751, cross-cohort replication, robustness to structural controls, and an E-value exceeding 5.

## 4.8 Implications for practitioners

Four implications follow. **(1) Register-based behavioral surveillance.** After each athlete's age-14 season, flag those whose combined ages-13–14 competition count falls below a threshold — fully automatic, with no athlete contact. A threshold of < 10 meets flags ~26% of the cohort with PPV = 0.94, about 10 percentage points above the 84% base rate; < 5 meets (~9% flagged) suits focused outreach, < 15 (~43%) broader screening (Table 6). **(2) Flag again after age 15 using within-athlete change.** Level at age 14 and change from age 14 to 15 carry independent information (Section 3.5; Table 4), yielding a two-screen strategy: level at end of age 14, change at end of age 15. **(3) Lower barriers to the next competition cycle for flagged athletes.** Subsidize travel, waive fees, organize coach-accompanied transport, schedule low-stakes practice meets, and signal that participation is welcome regardless of qualifying performance — each acts on the cost side of the engagement balance (Scanlan et al., 1993, 2016). Coach and parent contact complements, but does not substitute for, lowering structural barriers. **(4) Be cautious about prescribing event diversification for retention.** Within-sport concentration predicted *higher* retention (§4.6); federations should not assume that diversification pressure within track and field aids retention in this age group. Behavioral surveillance complements commitment instruments such as the SCQ-2 (Scanlan et al., 2016): behavior identifies *whom* to reach; understanding *why* still requires conversation.

## 4.9 Limitations

First, the outcome indexes *attrition from competition*, not from sport: an athlete who stops competing in track and field but continues to train, or moves to another sport, is counted as a dropout; the signal is best read as disengagement from organized competitive participation and the *competitive identity* it supports. Second, the register records official performance, not subjective experience; the qualitative literature remains essential for unpacking *why* volume declines. Third, the population is soft-selected through registered club membership: findings describe disengagement *from a baseline of demonstrable engagement* and do not generalize to children with no organized-sport history. Fourth, predictor and outcome share a measurement scale; despite the baseline-only primary model, landmark and zero-volume sensitivities, and the level-versus-change decomposition, structural overlap remains a conceptual limitation of register-only designs. Fifth, the post-baseline Cox specification violated proportional hazards (Schoenfeld χ² = 220.77); period-specific estimates are therefore reported descriptively. Sixth, the HHI treats event categories symmetrically although they co-occur unevenly in practice — at baseline, 98% of athletes with hurdles results also had sprint results, whereas throws overlapped less with other categories — so identical HHI values can represent somewhat different combination patterns; the direction of the specialization association was nonetheless robust across cohorts. Seventh, the youngest cohort has at most four post-senior years of observation; longer follow-up would help distinguish late dropouts from genuine career athletes.

## 4.10 Future directions

Priorities are linking the register-based behavioral signal to commitment instruments (e.g., SCQ-2) in currently active athletes, testing cross-sport generalization where registers are less complete, and quasi-experimental implementation of surveillance-triggered outreach to athletes with low but non-zero pre-milestone volume (e.g., 1 ≤ vol < 10).

## 4.11 Conclusion

Senior retention in Norwegian youth track and field is prospectively associated with baseline-window competition behavior: athletes who already compete more at ages 13–14 retain at substantially higher rates, and those who decline across the age-14-to-15 transition retain at substantially lower rates. The effect withstands structural controls, replicates across two birth-year cohorts, and is not driven by mechanical zero-values; early heterogeneity and within-athlete pull-back contribute independently. The findings are consistent with — though they do not directly confirm — long-standing accounts of dropout as a gradual deliberative process, and they suggest a practical role for prospective register-based behavioral surveillance, implementable at the end of an athlete's 14-year-old season.
