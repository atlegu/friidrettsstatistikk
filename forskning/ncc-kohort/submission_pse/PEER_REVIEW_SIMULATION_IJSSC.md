## Overall Assessment

### Recommendation

**Minor Revision**

### Confidence Score

**4 / 5** (expert in youth-sport participation and coaching practice; not the assigned statistical reviewer)

### Summary Assessment

This single-author register-based cohort study follows 2,123 Norwegian youth track-and-field athletes for up to 14 years and shows that competition behavior at ages 13–14 — principally the number of meets entered — prospectively discriminates senior retention (OR = 2.40 per SD; cross-validated AUC = 0.751), replicates across two birth cohorts, decomposes into independent baseline-heterogeneity and within-athlete pull-back components, and outperforms age-normed performance measured in the same window. From the editor's chair, the fit with IJSSC is excellent: the paper converts a long-theorized withdrawal process into an operational early-warning rule a federation can run from its own register, with explicit threshold trade-offs (Table 6) and a usable two-screen strategy (Section 4.8). The previous rejection at *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* turned mainly on theoretical framing, construct justification, and citation accuracy; my itemized check below finds those reports addressed point by point, including both citation errors, with only minor residue. What remains is editorial rather than structural: the practical implications treat senior retention as an unexamined good and headline a PPV inflated by an 84% non-retention base rate; the volume-as-choice assumption and register dependence need explicit boundary conditions for an international readership; and there are small internal inconsistencies (confidence intervals, an orphaned theory label in Figure 1, two cited tables absent from the file). All are fixable in text. I recommend Minor Revision.

## Strengths

**S1. Population-complete longitudinal behavioral data in a literature that has structurally lacked them.** The cohort is the full eligible population (Section 2.1), not a sample: 2,123 athletes, 230,868 competition entries, follow-up to 14 years with all athletes observed past age 20 (Sections 2.2–2.3). This directly answers the two structural problems the paper itself names (Section 1.2, Section 4.7): withdrawn athletes are systematically hard to recruit into surveys, and retrospective accounts compress a continuous process into a remembered decision. The claim that the trajectory of registered competition behavior "has not, to our knowledge, been examined" (Section 1.2) is, to my knowledge of this literature, correct — this is a genuinely new evidence type for the dropout field.

**S2. Design discipline against the obvious circularity objection.** The primary model uses only ages-13–14 predictors (Section 2.5.1, Table 3), avoiding look-ahead; the behavior-versus-performance comparison is fully time-aligned (Section 3.6: AUC 0.740 for volume vs 0.607 for Tyrving, both measured at 13–14); the level-versus-change decomposition (Table 4: OR = 2.79 for age-14 level, OR = 2.44 for 14→15 change; pseudo-R² 0.113 → 0.227) cleanly separates early heterogeneity from within-athlete pull-back; and the two-cohort replication (Section 3.10: OR = 2.22 and 2.79) is built into the design. Pre-registered-style internal replication is rare in this literature and materially raises my confidence.

**S3. Translation for exactly this journal's readership.** Section 4.8 and Table 6 deliver what IJSSC exists to publish: a threshold menu applied at the end of the age-14 season with stated trade-offs (< 5 meets for focused outreach, < 10 as the default, < 15 for broad screening), a two-screen strategy (level at 14, change at 15), concrete barrier-lowering actions, and an explicitly restrained reading of the specialization finding ("neither a license to push early specialization nor grounds for forced diversification," Section 4.6). The closing formulation — behavior identifies *whom* to reach, conversation identifies *why* — is genuinely useful to coaches and federations.

**S4. Calibrated interpretation and candour.** The paper repeatedly restrains its own claims: competition behavior is "an empirically observable behavioral indicator — not a direct measure of motivation" (Section 1.3); the epistemic-asymmetry paragraph (Section 4.2) concedes that "behavior outperforms performance" describes temporal structure, not mechanism; limitations are specific and quantified (98% hurdles–sprint co-occurrence; Schoenfeld χ² = 220.77 disclosed rather than buried; Section 4.9). AI assistance is transparently declared. This is the interpretive tone I want in the journal.

## Weaknesses

**W1. The practical implications treat senior retention as an unexamined good.**
**Problem:** Section 4.8 recommends register-based flagging and outreach toward athletes unlikely to retain senior status, but the paper never asks whether senior retention is the right normative target. Battaglia et al. (2024) — whose thesis is that the dropout-crisis framing is "not as simple as it appears" — is used only to fix terminology (Section 1), not engaged substantively. The paper's own summary of Espedalen (2025) describes a majority who participate "for more casual enjoyment and who readily reprioritize" (Section 1.1), which implies much non-retention is benign reprioritization rather than a problem to be fixed. The register also shows the steepest exit at age 17 (Section 3.2), raising whether adolescent exit, rather than senior continuation, is the practically meaningful target.
**Why it matters:** For a coaching and federation readership, Section 4.8 is the payload. Without a stated boundary on when retention support is appropriate, the surveillance recommendation can be read as licensing pressure on adolescents making reasonable life choices, and federations may misdirect scarce outreach resources.
**Suggestion:** Add a short paragraph to Section 4.8 or 4.9 that (a) engages Battaglia et al.'s substantive caution, (b) distinguishes retention-*supportive* barrier-lowering from retention *pressure*, and (c) narrows the intervention target to the low-but-nonzero group the author already identifies in Section 4.10 (1 ≤ vol < 10).
**Severity:** Major

**W2. The headline screening metric overstates practical gain because of the base rate.**
**Problem:** The abstract and Section 3.8 headline PPV ≈ 0.94, but with an 84% non-retention base rate PPV is nearly saturated. The abstract omits both the base rate and the sensitivity (0.30 at the featured < 10 threshold), and Table 6's NPV column (0.17–0.25) shows the *unflagged* group also mostly fails to retain. The manuscript does acknowledge the base-rate issue in the Table 6 note and Section 3.8 — but not where readers will act on it.
**Why it matters:** Coaches and federation analysts will quote the abstract's "PPV ≈ 0.94." A screen presented as 94% precise reads very differently when the unscreened expectation is already 84%.
**Suggestion:** Re-express Table 6 (or add a column) as retention among flagged vs unflagged athletes — at the < 10 threshold this is roughly 6% vs 22%, an intuitive and honest contrast; add the base-rate caveat and the sensitivity figure to the abstract; and state in Section 4.8 what the ~10-point precision gain buys per 100 athletes screened.
**Severity:** Major

**W3. Boundary conditions for transfer beyond Norway are implicit, not stated.**
**Problem:** The licence to read competition volume as an engagement choice rests on Norway-specific structure (Section 2.2: open entry, no coach gatekeeping, club-paid entry fees, short travel), and the recommendations presuppose a complete national register. Section 4.9 covers soft selection into the cohort but not cross-context transferability; Section 4.10 only gestures at "registers... less complete."
**Why it matters:** IJSSC's readership is international. In coach-gated or fee-heavy systems (much of North America and the UK), a low meet count measures access and selection, not engagement, and the early-warning rule would misfire if imported uncritically.
**Suggestion:** Add an explicit boundary-conditions passage to the discussion (athlete-controlled entry, low marginal cost of entry, register completeness as prerequisites for transfer) and temper the generalized phrasing "clubs and federations" in the abstract and Section 4.8 accordingly.
**Severity:** Minor

**W4. Internal consistency and completeness of the submission package.**
**Problem:** (a) The abstract and Section 3.3 report the volume CI as [2.07, 2.78], but Table 3 gives [2.08, 2.76]. (b) The HHI OR appears as 1.35 (Sections 3.3, 3.9, 4.6) and 1.36 (Table 3; and Section 4.6's "OR = 1.32 vs. 1.36"). (c) Table 5 and Table 7 are cited (Sections 3.7, 3.10, 4.1) but are not present in the reviewed file. (d) Figure 1's caption invokes self-determination theory, which is neither developed nor referenced anywhere in the main text.
**Why it matters:** Numeric inconsistencies erode reader trust in an otherwise meticulous paper; missing tables would halt production; the orphaned SDT label re-opens a criticism the prior referee already made about superficial theory name-dropping.
**Suggestion:** Reconcile every duplicated statistic against the analysis output; include Tables 5 and 7 in the submission; delete the SDT mention from the Figure 1 caption or support it with a citation and a sentence in Sections 1.1/4.4.
**Severity:** Minor

## Overall Assessment

### Recommendation

**Major Revision**

### Confidence Score

**4 / 5** (high confidence in the statistical assessment; full verification limited because analysis code is promised only "upon acceptance" and several supplementary estimates could not be traced to a documented model specification).

### Summary Assessment

This is a methodologically serious register cohort study (n = 2,123, up to 14 years of follow-up) whose core design decision — a baseline-only (ages 13–14) logistic model for senior retention, with cross-validated discrimination, structural controls, cross-cohort replication, and a level-vs-change decomposition — directly and largely successfully answers the circularity concern that dogged the previous submission. The trajectory descriptives, the time-aligned behavior-vs-performance comparison (Supplementary Table S15), and the transparent overlap caveats on post-baseline specifications are exemplary for this literature. However, the manuscript is not yet publishable in its present form. I identified: (1) internal numerical inconsistencies between abstract, text, main tables, and supplement, including a supplementary sensitivity table (S9) that reports OR = 3.08 for the *primary outcome* where the primary model reports 2.40, and an E-value table (S3) whose input hazard ratios (0.35; Tyrving 0.86) appear nowhere else in the manuscript; (2) an E-value computed on the overlap-contaminated milestone-window specification but rhetorically attached to the baseline-only headline result (§3.10, §4.7); (3) inadequate missing-data handling for the primary model (complete-case with a Cox-only mean-imputation check; the n = 1,914 pull-back model is arithmetically hard to reconcile with ~20% Tyrving missingness); (4) an unaddressed mechanical dependence of the HHI on result count that plausibly contributes to the L3-null/L4-significant sign pattern; and (5) prediction-reporting gaps (no model calibration, no CIs on threshold metrics, mutually inconsistent rows in Table 6, random forest declared but never reported in the Results narrative). All are fixable without new data collection.

## Strengths

**S1. The baseline-only primary design genuinely neutralizes look-ahead in the headline estimate.** The primary model (§2.5.1, Table 3) uses only ages-13–14 predictors for an outcome first observable at age 20+, leaving a multi-year buffer; the explicit rationale ("an athlete who drops out before age 15 has milestone-window volume of zero by construction") shows the authors understand exactly what the earlier design got wrong. The time-aligned comparison (§3.6, Table S15: sex + Tyrving AUC 0.607 vs. sex + ages-13–14 volume 0.740, both measured in the same window) removes the temporal-advantage confound that a prior referee could reasonably have suspected, and Supplementary Table S11 (excluding 589 zero-milestone-volume athletes; HR 0.51) plus the landmark analysis (Table S8, HR 0.60) show the post-baseline association is not purely mechanical. This is the correct analytic architecture for the question.

**S2. The level-vs-change decomposition (§3.5, Table 4) is an elegant and correctly interpreted analysis.** Restricting to athletes still active at age 14 and entering level (OR 2.79) and within-athlete change (OR 2.44) jointly, with pseudo-R² rising 0.113 → 0.227, cleanly separates "behavioral typology" from "pull-back" — the two readings of Figure 2 — instead of letting the trajectory plot carry an ambiguous claim. Few papers in this literature formalize that distinction.

**S3. Replication and robustness apparatus is unusually extensive and mostly honestly labeled.** Cross-cohort re-estimation (Table 7: OR 2.22 vs 2.79, both p < .001), structural controls with a stable coefficient (Table S13: OR 2.40 with and without controls), cluster-robust SEs (S2), outcome-definition sensitivity (S9), and explicit "descriptive, not prospective" caveats on the overlapping specifications (Table 5 note, Table S17 note, Figure 3 caption) demonstrate self-aware handling of the design's structural limits. The Table 5 period decomposition (HR 0.14 → 0.69 → 0.96) is the right way to display the PH violation rather than hide it.

**S4. Measurement definitions and base-rate honesty are strong.** Variable construction is operationally explicit (§2.4.1–2.4.5; combined-event counting resolved; de-duplication and club-transfer handling stated, §2.2), and Table 6's note openly concedes that PPV ≈ 0.94 sits on an 84% non-retention base rate, quantifying the increment (9–12 points) rather than letting the raw PPV impress. The detection-capacity analysis (S-M2) replacing boilerplate a-priori power is conceptually appropriate for a full-population register design, and the Hsieh & Lavori arithmetic checks out (1,570 events → HR_min ≈ 1.07 per SD).

## Weaknesses

**W1. Internal numerical inconsistencies, including supplementary estimates that cannot be traced to any documented model.**
**Problem:** (a) Abstract and §3.3 report the headline CI as [2.07, 2.78]; Table 3 reports [2.08, 2.76]; Table S13 reports [2.08, 2.78] — three variants of the same estimate. (b) HHI is 1.36 [1.17, 1.57] in Table 3 but cited as 1.35 [1.16, 1.57] in §3.3, §3.9, and §4.6 (1.35 [1.16, 1.56] in S13) — the text appears to quote the controls-adjusted model while calling it "the primary regression." (c) Table S9 lists outcome B as "primary" with OR = 3.08 and AUC = 0.86, but the primary model for that same outcome (Table 3) gives OR = 2.40 and CV-AUC = 0.751; the S9 "full model" is evidently a different, undocumented specification — which defeats the purpose of an outcome-definition sensitivity for the primary estimate. (d) Table S3's E-value inputs are untraceable: volume HR = 0.35 [0.33, 0.38] matches no other reported model (S2/S5 give 0.44, S8 0.60, S11 0.51 — and robust SEs do not change point estimates, so S2's 0.44 should equal the base post-baseline model); Tyrving "per SD, M2" HR = 0.86 [0.82, 0.90] appears nowhere else (Tyrving HRs elsewhere are 0.99–1.07), and "M2" is undefined in the supplement.
**Why it matters:** Reviewers and readers must be able to reconcile every number; untraceable inputs to the robustness analyses undermine confidence in the entire results pipeline and raise the question of stale results from earlier manuscript versions surviving into the supplement.
**Suggestion:** Re-run the final pipeline end-to-end from a single script; regenerate every table and in-text statistic from one source of truth; for each supplementary table state the exact model (covariate set, sample, n). Explicitly label which model each in-text OR/CI quotes. If S9's sensitivity uses a different covariate set, either re-do it on the primary L4 specification or say precisely what was fitted.
**Severity:** Major (escalates toward Critical if the S3/S9 numbers cannot be reproduced from a documented specification).

**W2. The E-value is computed for the demoted, overlap-contaminated specification but is presented as robustness of the headline finding.**
**Problem:** §3.10's sentence reads "The effect was robust … to unmeasured confounding: E-value = 5.16 … for the milestone-window volume effect" — where "the effect" in that paragraph is the *pre-milestone* (baseline) volume effect. §4.7 then lists "a baseline-only signal with AUC = 0.751, cross-cohort replication, robustness to structural controls, and an E-value exceeding 5" as one package. But the E-value is computed on the ages-15–16 volume HR (S3: 0.35) — precisely the specification the paper itself demotes for predictor–outcome overlap (§3.7, Table 5 note), and one that violates PH so severely (Schoenfeld χ² = 220.77) that a single pooled HR is a questionable input to any E-value.
**Why it matters:** The unmeasured-confounding robustness claim for the primary baseline estimate is currently unsupported. Using the baseline-only Cox HR (S16: 0.49 per SD) gives an E-value around 3.5, not >5; for the logistic OR 2.40 with a 16.4% outcome, the OR must first be converted to an approximate RR (the outcome is not rare) before E-value computation — the honest number will be materially smaller than 5.16.
**Suggestion:** Compute and report the E-value for the *primary* estimate (baseline-only specification; OR→RR conversion per VanderWeele & Ding for a common outcome, or use the S16 baseline Cox HR), present the milestone-window E-value only alongside its overlap caveat, and rewrite §3.10 and §4.7 so the robustness attributions match the specifications they were computed on.
**Severity:** Major.

**W3. Missing-data handling is inadequate for the primary analysis, and the pull-back model's n is unexplained.**
**Problem:** 419/2,123 athletes (19.7%) are dropped from the primary model (n = 1,704), with Tyrving missing "for athletes whose baseline events could not be scored" (S-M3) — a mechanism plausibly related to event profile and engagement, i.e., not MCAR. The only sensitivity is mean imputation, and Table S5 applies it *only to the Cox specification* (columns are HRs); the primary logistic model has no missing-data sensitivity at all. No comparison of included vs. excluded athletes is provided (a STROBE expectation). Separately, Table 4's model includes Tyrving yet reports n = 1,914 — the full count of athletes active at age 14. With ~20% Tyrving missingness, a complete-case fit should yield roughly 1,500–1,600 unless missingness is concentrated among the early-inactive or Tyrving was imputed here; neither is stated. Handling of the 24 sex-unknown athletes is also never specified.
**Why it matters:** Complete-case estimates are biased when missingness depends on outcome-related factors; mean imputation understates uncertainty and cannot serve as the sole check; and an unexplained analysis n invites the suspicion of inconsistent samples across tables.
**Suggestion:** Add multiple imputation (e.g., MICE with the outcome in the imputation model) as the principal sensitivity for the primary logistic model; add a table comparing complete vs. incomplete cases on key covariates and the outcome; state explicitly the sample construction for Table 4 (and why n = 1,914 is attainable with Tyrving in the model); state how sex-unknown athletes were handled; state that L1–L3 were fitted on the fixed L4 complete-case sample (as the constant n = 1,704 implies) for AUC comparability.
**Severity:** Major.

**W4. The HHI is mechanically bounded by result count, and the L3-null → L4-significant sign pattern is not distinguished from a conditioning artifact.**
**Problem:** HHI = Σs², computed on shares of an athlete's *results* (§2.4.3). An athlete with one result has HHI = 1 by construction; with n results, HHI ≥ 1/min(n, k). Low-activity athletes are therefore forced toward apparent "concentration," making HHI mechanically negatively associated with volume. This structure alone can produce exactly the observed pattern: unconditionally, high HHI is entangled with low volume (which predicts dropout), suppressing the coefficient toward null (L3: OR 1.09, p = .238); conditioning on volume removes the mechanical component and the coefficient jumps to 1.36 (L4). Table S18 rules out performance confounding — a good analysis — but nothing addresses count-dependence, and the HHI–volume (or HHI–result count) correlation is never reported. The §3.9/§4.6 substantive interpretation ("concentration is protective") and practitioner implication (4) rest on this coefficient.
**Why it matters:** If the L4 HHI effect partly reflects residual small-count geometry rather than a specialization choice, the paper's most counterintuitive secondary finding — and a practitioner recommendation — could be an artifact of the measure's construction.
**Suggestion:** (i) Report correlations of HHI with result count and meet count; (ii) refit L4 restricted to athletes with enough results for HHI to vary meaningfully (e.g., ≥5 and ≥8 baseline results); (iii) use a finite-sample-corrected concentration index (e.g., (HHI − 1/n)/(1 − 1/n)) as a sensitivity; (iv) temper §3.9/§4.6 and implication (4) accordingly, and discuss the sign flip explicitly as mutual-adjustment behavior rather than reporting it only in a table note.
**Severity:** Major.

**W5. Prediction-model reporting falls short of TRIPOD expectations: no calibration, no uncertainty on threshold metrics, internally inconsistent Table 6 rows, and a declared-but-unreported random forest.**
**Problem:** (a) The word "calibration" (§3.8, S-M4, Table 6 note) is used for classification metrics; actual model calibration (calibration slope/intercept or a calibration plot for the L4 predicted probabilities) is reported nowhere, although the model is framed as a prediction/surveillance tool. (b) Table 6 metrics carry no confidence intervals and are in-sample. (c) The five metrics per row of Table 6 are not mutually consistent: e.g., at threshold <10, flagged 26.4%, PPV 0.94, and sensitivity 0.30 jointly imply specificity ≈ 0.90 and NPV ≈ 0.20, not the reported 0.92 and 0.22 (similar ~2-point drift at <15); either denominators differ across columns or an error has crept in. (d) The abstract advertises PPV ≈ 0.94 without the accompanying sensitivity of 0.30 — 70% of eventual non-retainers are never flagged, which materially qualifies the "surveillance" claim. (e) The random forest is declared in §2.5.5 ("convergent variable-importance evidence") but never appears in the Results narrative; Tables S12/S17 are uncited from the main text; the importance type (Gini vs. permutation) is unstated; hyperparameters (500 trees, depth 8) are unjustified; S12's top-ranked features (volume at 16, milestone composite, Tyrving slope 13–16) are post-baseline/look-ahead variables, so S12 cannot serve as convergent evidence for the *baseline* claim without saying so; and S17's n = 1,217 is a third, unexplained analysis sample. (f) Declared variables tyrving_mean and res_age_X (§2.4.2, §2.4.4) are never reported anywhere.
**Why it matters:** A screening recommendation to federations needs calibrated probabilities, uncertainty, and arithmetic that closes; declared-but-unreported analyses and variables raise selective-reporting concerns.
**Suggestion:** Report a calibration plot or slope/intercept for the L4 model (ideally cross-validated); add bootstrap CIs to Table 6 and recompute the rows on one stated denominator; add sensitivity to the abstract sentence; either report the RF in Results (with importance type, tuning rationale, seed, and a baseline-features-only importance run) or delete it from Methods; report or remove tyrving_mean and res_age_X.
**Severity:** Major (none of W1–W5 is Critical because the core design and headline association appear sound; collectively they mandate major revision).

**Why it matters:** This attribution is load-bearing for H1 and for the §4.4 integration. After the previous round's citation problem, an attribution a domain referee already failed to verify cannot be left unanchored.

**Severity: Major**

**W2. Citation-accuracy residue, including one verified mischaracterization introduced in revision.**
**Problem:** (a) §4.6 states "markers of early specialization predicted burnout and dropout in youth swimming (Larson et al., 2019)." **W3. Missing nearest-neighbor literatures; the contribution claim needs sharpening to survive.**
**Problem:** §1.2 claims "The trajectory of registered competition behavior … has not, to our knowledge, been examined." **Severity: Major**

**W4. The interpretive license "volume = engagement choice" needs documentation, and one rival explanation — injury — is entirely missing.**
**Problem:** §2.2's institutional claims ("no qualifying standards, no roster selection, no coach-controlled gatekeeping"; club-paid entry fees) carry the paper's central interpretive move ("which is what licenses interpreting competition volume at ages 13–14 as a behavioral indicator of engagement"), yet cite no documentary source. §4.2 works through cost and deliberate periodization as alternative causes of low/declining volume — but never injury. **Suggestion:** Cite the federation's competition regulations for §2.2's claims and add a footnote reconciling selected-team events; add an injury paragraph to §4.2 and the limitations (ideally probing whether the register can partially discriminate, e.g., mid-season truncation versus season-long absence); temper "primarily expresses the athlete's own choice" accordingly; state the regional coverage in §2.2 or §4.9.
**Severity: Major**

### Introduction and theory

The introduction now has a coherent arc (attrition scale → definitions → process accounts → quantitative gap → present study), and §1.3's hypothesis derivations are a real improvement over the PSE version. Three residual issues. ## Overall Assessment

### Recommendation

**Major Revision**

### Confidence Score

**4 / 5** — expert confidence within my lane (policy, governance, implementation); I deliberately abstain from judging the statistics.

### Summary Assessment

This is an unusually strong empirical contribution attached to an underdeveloped and, in one respect, ethically under-examined practical program. The register design is genuinely novel, the institutional description of Norwegian open-entry athletics (§2.2) is exemplary and gives practitioners exactly the contextual information needed to judge whether "competition volume = athlete choice" is credible, and the results sections are candid about the base-rate arithmetic behind the headline PPV. The paper convincingly answers the practice-relevant objections raised at PSE (athlete control over entries, cost/SES confounding, taper strategies).

However, §4.8 proposes that federations operate automated "behavioral surveillance" of 13–14-year-olds — "fully automatic, with no athlete contact" — and the manuscript's ethics apparatus covers only the *research* use of the register, never the *operational* system it recommends. The prediction-to-intervention leap is under-hedged; the retention-maximization objective is adopted without argument despite the paper's own admission that register dropout is not welfare-relevant harm; the targeted-versus-universal question is never posed even though NPV = 0.22 means unflagged athletes still fail to retain at ~78%; and no costs, actors, pilot design, or scope conditions are given. All of these are fixable by rewriting §§4.8–4.10 and the ethics statements, without new data — hence Major Revision rather than rejection. (198 words)

## Strengths

**S1. The institutional analysis in §2.2 is a model of what applied sport science needs.** The paragraph explaining that Norwegian youth athletics is open-entry, that "athletes — together with their families — decide which meets and events to enter," that clubs ordinarily pay entry fees, and that the first selective gate (UM) arrives at 15, is precisely the information a coach or policy-maker needs to judge whether volume can be read as engagement. It also directly and successfully answers prior Referee 2's central question ("How much control do youth athletes have over the number of competitions they attend?"). Few papers in this literature ground their measurement interpretation in the actual entry rules of the system studied.

**S2. Base-rate candor in the results.** §3.8 and the Table 6 note explicitly state that "the high PPV partly reflects the 84% non-retention base rate" and quantify the increment as 9–12 percentage points; §4.8(1) repeats the base-rate context. Screening papers routinely bury this; here the results section discloses it, and the five-threshold menu with explicit sensitivity/coverage trade-offs treats practitioners as adults rather than selling a single magic cut-point.

**S3. The intervention direction is structural, not deficit-based.** §4.8(3) proposes lowering barriers (fees, travel, low-stakes meets, welcome signals) rather than "fixing" the athlete — consistent with the SES evidence the paper cites (Espedalen & Seippel, 2024) and with good practice in youth policy. Likewise, §4.8(4)'s refusal to convert the specialization finding into prescription ("neither a license to push early specialization nor grounds for advocating forced diversification") and the closing line — "behavior identifies *whom* to reach; understanding *why* still requires conversation" — show real restraint.

**S4. The paper already contains the seeds of proper hedging.** §4.10 proposes "quasi-experimental implementation of surveillance-triggered outreach," sensibly restricted to athletes with low but non-zero volume (1 ≤ vol < 10), and limitation 1 in §4.9 concedes that a register "dropout" may be thriving in another sport. These are the right instincts; the revision mostly needs to move them upstream into §4.8 so the recommendation is framed by them rather than merely followed by them.

## Weaknesses

**W1. The proposed operational surveillance of minors receives no ethical or governance analysis.**
**Problem:** §4.8(1) recommends federations "flag" 13–14-year-olds "fully automatic, with no athlete contact"; §4.11 repeats that surveillance is "implementable at the end of an athlete's 14-year-old season." The ethics material (§2.7; Statements: "Ethics committee approval was not required…"; "no participants were contacted or recruited") addresses only the research use under Helseforskningsloven §4. Nothing addresses the operational system: an operational flag is algorithmic profiling of children (GDPR Art. 4(4)) on data collected for a different purpose (competition administration → risk scoring; purpose limitation, Art. 5(1)(b)); "no athlete contact" collides with transparency duties (Arts. 12–14) and the right to object (Art. 21); children's data trigger heightened protection (Recital 38; cf. UNCRC General Comment No. 25), and systematic scoring of minors is a classic DPIA trigger (Art. 35). Nor is flag *visibility* discussed: in a volunteer club, the person who "acts on the flag" is the coach — and coach-expectancy research gives clear reasons to fear labeling and self-fulfilling-prophecy effects ("they're leaving anyway"). Finally, because the register holds no SES data (§4.2), a federation could not even audit the system for disparate impact, although the paper's own cited evidence implies flags will correlate with family income.
**Why it matters:** As written, a federation board could cite this paper as academic warrant for an unexamined child-profiling system. In a coaching journal, that is a foreseeable and consequential use.
**Suggestion:** Add a "governance preconditions" paragraph (or subsection) to §4.8: identified legal basis plus DPIA before deployment; flags visible only to a designated club/federation officer, not routinely to coaches; transparent communication to families with an opt-out; defined retention/sunset for flag data; equity monitoring. Reframe "fully automatic, with no athlete contact" from a selling point into a recognized transparency problem.
**Severity: Critical** (fully fixable by rewriting; no new data required).

**W2. The leap from prediction to intervention — and the unexamined objective of retention-maximization.**
**Problem:** The study demonstrates prediction only, yet §4.8 moves directly to "flag and intervene." Three internal tensions go unaddressed. (i) The paper's own framework describes a large casual majority who "readily reprioritize when sport becomes more demanding" (§1.1, Espedalen, 2025): many flagged athletes will have *chosen* lower engagement, for whom retention outreach is at best wasted and at worst unwelcome pressure arriving mid-way through what Eliasson and Johansson describe as an emotionally demanding exit process. (ii) Limitation 1 concedes register dropout ≠ leaving sport — so PPV = 0.94 is precision against *register exit*, not against welfare-relevant harm; a 14-year-old flourishing in handball is a true positive for the register and a false positive for public health, and §4.8 never carries this through. (iii) There is a coherence problem between §2.2 and §4.8(3): the interpretation of volume as choice rests on §2.2's claim that clubs pay fees and travel distances are short — yet the flagship remedies are "subsidize travel, waive fees." If those barriers are as low as §2.2 argues, those two remedies have little headroom (the low-stakes-meets and welcome-signal items, which act on psychological costs, remain coherent).
**Why it matters:** A federation's objective (retain members) and the public-health objective (keep adolescents active anywhere) diverge; the paper silently adopts the former. And interventions justified by a barrier mechanism the design itself argues away will not survive a board's scrutiny.
**Suggestion:** State the normative premise explicitly; distinguish blocked-but-willing athletes from athletes exercising choice, acknowledge the register cannot tell them apart, and conclude — explicitly — that any intervention must therefore be a low-pressure *offer*, never persistence pressure. Reconcile the §2.2/§4.8(3) barrier tension. Promote the §4.10 quasi-experiment from afterthought to the central practical implication: the honest headline is "here is a testable targeting rule," not "here is a surveillance system."
**Severity: Major**

**W3. The targeted-screening logic is never compared with the universal alternative, and the screen's limits as a screen are not confronted.**
**Problem:** At the recommended threshold, sensitivity = 0.30 and NPV = 0.22 (Table 6): the system misses 70% of future non-retainers, and *unflagged* athletes still fail to retain at ~78%. This is not a screen separating at-risk from safe; it re-ranks an almost uniformly high-risk population (94% vs. 78% absolute risk). Two consequences are ignored. First, false reassurance: a club that treats unflagged athletes as "safe" will be wrong three times in four; nowhere does the text warn against this misreading, and the NPV column is never interpreted. Second, since the proposed interventions (lower fees, more low-stakes meets, transport) are classic *universal* provisions, a board will immediately ask: why not provide them population-wide and skip profiling — avoiding every cost identified in W1? The paper never poses the question.
**Why it matters:** The comparison that decides real resource-allocation debates — targeted versus universal, given intervention cost — is absent, so §4.8 cannot actually guide the decision it addresses.
**Suggestion:** Report the flagged-vs-unflagged absolute risks (94% vs. 78%) alongside PPV in §4.8; add an explicit sentence that unflagged status is not reassurance; add a short decision-analytic paragraph stating under what cost structure targeting dominates universal provision (cheap universal provision + expensive per-athlete outreach is the plausible boundary), and acknowledge universal barrier-lowering as the profiling-free alternative.
**Severity: Major**

**W4. Implementation realism and transferability are asserted, not analyzed.**
**Problem:** §4.8 names no actors (who generates flags — the federation, which holds the register? who receives them — clubs? who funds "subsidized travel"?), no costs, no pilot or evaluation design, and no account of capacity in volunteer-run clubs (a volunteer coach handed a list of ten flagged names has been given work, not help). Sharing a minor's risk flag from federation to club is itself a data-transfer event (see W1). Transferability is similarly unexamined: the interpretive license and the specific threshold (<10 meets across two seasons) are products of Norwegian conditions — complete national register, open entry, club-pays-fees, dense meet calendar (§2.2). In pay-to-play systems the same volume measure is heavily resource-confounded, and the threshold numbers do not travel. §4.10's nod to "registers [that] are less complete" is not a substitute for stated scope conditions.
**Why it matters:** IJSSC's readership is practitioners; an implications section that cannot answer "who does what, at what cost, and how will we know it worked" will not move a federation board — and uncritical export of the thresholds to other systems is a foreseeable misuse.
**Suggestion:** Add one paragraph sketching a minimal viable implementation (federation-generated flags; one designated recipient per club; a menu of provisions; simple monitoring of flag→offer→uptake), an honest statement that costs and intervention effects are unknown pending the §4.10 pilot, and an explicit scope-conditions sentence: the rule is calibrated to systems with complete registers, open entry, and club-borne fees; elsewhere both interpretation and thresholds require re-derivation.
**Severity: Major**

**W5. The practitioner-facing surfaces are less candid than the results.**
**Problem:** The abstract states the rule "flags high-risk athletes with PPV ≈ 0.94, giving clubs and federations an early-warning window of several years for retention-supporting intervention" — no base rate, no sensitivity, and an "early-warning window *for* intervention" phrasing that presumes intervention efficacy the study does not test. Table 6's note and §4.8(1) show the authors know better; the strongest claims travel farthest (abstracts are what busy practitioners read) with the least context.
**Why it matters:** In a coaching journal, the abstract *is* the knowledge-translation product.
**Suggestion:** Amend to something like "flags athletes at high risk of non-retention (PPV ≈ 0.94 against an 84% base rate; sensitivity 0.30), creating a multi-year window in which retention-supporting intervention could be tested."
**Severity: Minor** (one-sentence fix, but mandatory).

## Detailed Comments

### §4.8, implication by implication

- **(1) "Register-based behavioral surveillance."** The base-rate framing here is honest (credit where due), but "fully automatic, with no athlete contact" is presented as a feature when it is also the core governance problem (W1). I would also gently suggest the term "monitoring" for practitioner-facing text — or, if the authors keep "surveillance," they should own its implications rather than use it innocently; in my field the word is a claim, not a synonym.
- **(2) The two-screen strategy** is operationally the most convincing part: it maps onto real federation season calendars, requires only data the federation already holds, and the level/change decomposition (Table 4) gives it a defensible rationale. Note for the revision: the second screen (end of age 15) fires *after* the first selective gate (UM), so its downstream interventions target ages 16+, where the KM curves show the steepest attrition (age 17) — worth one sentence making that timing logic explicit, because it is actually a point in the proposal's favor.
- **(3) Barrier-lowering.** See W2(iii) for the §2.2 tension. Additionally: "coach-accompanied transport" and outreach to families implicate parental communication and consent that the paper never mentions, despite parents appearing in its own theoretical sources (Eliasson & Johansson interviewed parents).
- **(4) Caution on diversification prescriptions** is well-judged and correctly bounded. No changes requested.
- **Missing from all four:** athletes' own voice. The entire pipeline treats the young athlete as a data object; nothing asks whether 13–15-year-olds would regard being flagged as acceptable (UNCRC Art. 12 is the standard anchor here). A stakeholder-consultation step (athletes' council, parent representatives, club volunteers) belongs in the pilot design of §4.10. Relatedly, multi-sport clubs — common in Norway — are a blind spot: a flagged athlete may be increasing handball activity *within the same club*; club-level actors will know this, the federation's flag generator will not, which both inflates welfare-irrelevant flags and weakens clubs' incentive to act.

### Ethics and Statements sections

§2.7 and the Statements are adequate for the research conducted — the public-register/Helseforskningsloven §4 reasoning is standard and the GDPR handling of the derived dataset is described responsibly. My concern (W1) is what is absent: the research-ethics exemption is silently allowed to stand in for the legitimacy of the *operational* system §4.8 recommends, and these are different questions under different legal regimes. One explicit sentence acknowledging that deployment by a federation would require its own legal basis, DPIA, and transparency arrangements — i.e., that this paper licenses none of that — would resolve the ambiguity.

### Limitations (§4.9) from an applied view

The seven limitations are all epistemic (outcome boundary, no subjective data, soft selection, shared measurement scale, PH violation, HHI symmetry, follow-up length). Not one concerns the practical proposal: no limitation states that intervention effects are untested, that flagging carries labeling and data-protection risks, that the thresholds are system-specific, or that the register cannot separate blocked-but-willing from happily-departing athletes at the individual level. Given that §4.8 is the paper's practical payload, the limitations section should carry at least one paragraph of *applied* limitations. Limitation 1 is well written but stops one step short of its §4.8 consequence.

### The abstract's practitioner promises

Covered in W5. One further note: "early-warning window of several years for retention-supporting intervention" quietly converts an association into an intervention opportunity; the conclusion (§4.11) does the same ("suggest a practical role for prospective register-based behavioral surveillance") with no governance qualifier. Both sentences need the same softening as the abstract.

# Devil's Advocate Report

## Manuscript Information

- **Title:** Pulling back before dropping out: Behavioral disengagement precedes exit from Norwegian youth track and field — a 14-year register study
- **Target journal:** International Journal of Sports Science & Coaching (SAGE)
- **Review date:** 2026-08-25
- **Materials reviewed:** MANUSCRIPT_FULL.md (main text, Tables 1–4, 6, Figures 1–3); Supplementary Methods (S-M1–S-M4); prior Psychology of Sport and Exercise referee reports (Referee 1, Referee 2). Supplementary Tables S1–S16 and cited main-text Tables 5 and 7 were not present in the provided full manuscript file (see Issue 13).

## Strongest Counter-Argument

The headline claim — behavioral disengagement precedes exit — must survive two independent attacks, and it survives neither cleanly. First, the construct attack: predictor and outcome are the same register variable, registered competition frequency, measured at different ages. The paper concedes this in §4.2 ("predictor and outcome both index registered competitive activity, so 'behavior outperforms performance' describes the temporal structure through which disengagement becomes observable, not a causal mechanism") — but the concession gives up the mechanism while keeping the motivational label. §2.4.4 states volumes are "not direct measures of motivation or commitment"; the title nonetheless says "behavioral disengagement," a motivational construct. Second, the process attack: "pulling back before dropping out" is a within-athlete sequential claim, yet the evidence is (a) group medians across calendar age (Table 2), which a mixture of abrupt exits at heterogeneous ages can mimic — the IQR shows at least a quarter of future dropouts already at zero meets at age 15 — and (b) a 14→15 change coefficient (Table 4, OR 2.44) in which the largest "pull-backs" (14→0) *are* the exits, not their antecedents. No exit-aligned trajectory analysis is reported, and the one contamination-free analysis (landmark, §3.7) uses volume *level*, which is equally consistent with stable typology. Meanwhile the alternative that best fits the Norwegian context — multi-sport consolidation at ages 14–16, where declining track volume signals *rising* commitment elsewhere — is defined away in §1 yet contradicts the commitment-erosion reading throughout §4.4, and the abstract's first sentence still generalizes to "youth sport." What survives is valuable but different: a well-powered description of *when* trajectories diverge, plus a screening threshold with modest lift (PPV 0.94 against an 84% base rate, sensitivity 0.30). That paper is publishable. This framing is not yet.

## Issue List

**1. CRITICAL [logic / evidence] — Title, §3.2, §3.5, §4.4.** The individual-level process claim is never demonstrated. §4.4 asserts the register observes "two to three seasons of thinning participation preceding the final exit," but this is read off group medians by calendar age (Table 2: 8 → 8 → 3 → 0), and a median path across 1,775 dropouts with heterogeneous exit ages is not an individual path — the age-15 IQR [0–11] shows ≥25% of dropouts already at zero, i.e., abrupt exit after 14. The change analysis (Table 4) cannot rescue it: "volume change 14→15" scores an athlete going 14→0 as maximal pull-back, so the OR of 2.44 conflates the exit itself with an antecedent of exit; the landmark analysis (§3.7) is free of that contamination but uses level, not change, so it cannot distinguish pull-back from stable low engagement. Every reported estimate is therefore either typology-compatible (level) or exit-contaminated (change), and the title-level claim rests on an aggregate-to-individual inference the design has not earned. **Neutralize:** exit-aligned trajectories (volume at T−1, T−2, T−3 relative to each athlete's final season), report the percentage of dropouts with at least one reduced-but-nonzero season before their final season, and/or a change-based model among athletes who remained active through 16 predicting post-16 exit.

**2. MAJOR [framing / logic] — Title, Abstract, §2.4.4 vs. §4.2/§4.9.** The near-tautology is conceded but the label is kept. The primary model is genuinely prospective (6-year separation; no definitional dependence — credit in Observations), so the strong form of the tautology charge fails; what remains is that "registered competition frequency predicts registered competition frequency" is behavioral persistence, the least surprising regularity in behavioral science. The authors' own hedges — "not direct measures of motivation or commitment" (§2.4.4), "structural overlap remains a conceptual limitation of register-only designs" (§4.9), the §4.2 epistemic-asymmetry paragraph — are honest, but they are incompatible with a title that names the predictor "behavioral disengagement" and an abstract that presents temporal structure as the discovery of a withdrawal signature. Even the concession assumes the construct ("the temporal structure through which *disengagement* becomes observable"): what is conceded is causality; what is missing is construct validity. **Neutralize:** either validate the label against any external engagement measure (the authors' own §4.10 proposal), or retitle/reframe descriptively (e.g., "competition-frequency trajectories anticipate exit").

**3. MAJOR [evidence] — §3.6, §4.2, Table 3, S-M3.** "Behavior outperforms performance" (AUC 0.740 vs. 0.607) is confounded by differential measurement quality. Volume is a near-noiseless count over two full seasons; Tyrving best is a sampling maximum over few performances (mechanically coupled to volume itself), age-normed, capped at 1,500, and missing for ~20% — with missingness ("baseline events could not be scored") plausibly related to the predictor and outcome, handled by complete-case deletion. Under classical measurement error, the noisier predictor loses the AUC contest regardless of construct importance; no reliability estimate is reported for either variable. The within-stratum analyses and ρ = .29–.45 (§3.6) show volume is not reducible to performance, but they do not address the noise asymmetry. **Neutralize:** reliability estimates, errors-in-variables or SIMEX sensitivity, multiple imputation with a plausible missingness model, and an explicit caveat attached to the §4.2 heading.

**4. MAJOR [generalization / framing] — Abstract, §1.3, §3.10, §4.7.** "Replicated across two independent birth cohorts" is a split-sample consistency check, not replication. Cohorts A and B come from the same six editions of the same meet series at the same three venues (overlapping at 2014), the same register, the same clubs and coaches, and adjacent birth years; they also differ materially on follow-up (still active 2024+: 5.8% vs. 10.8%). This rules out sampling noise and cohort-specific shocks — the modest purpose §1.3 states — but the abstract's "independent" implies more, and §4.7 stacks "cross-cohort replication" rhetorically with AUC and E-value as compounding evidence. Note also that a persistence association (Issue 2) would replicate in *any* split, so replication does zero work against the deepest criticism. **Neutralize:** rename as internal split-sample consistency; temper the abstract.

**5. MAJOR [evidence / logic] — §3.10, §4.7, §2.5.4.** The E-value is computed for the wrong estimate and is inflated by the very overlap the paper disavows. The E-value of 5.16 belongs to the *milestone-window* (ages 15–16) volume effect — the specification §3.7 explicitly demotes ("not used as a primary estimate because predictor and at-risk window overlap"). An association that is partially definitional (quitting at 15 forces low milestone volume) needs no confounder to explain it, so E-value logic does not apply, and its impressive magnitude is an artifact of the overlap. §4.7 then transfers "an E-value exceeding 5" into the summary of register strengths alongside the *baseline-only* AUC, implying the robustness belongs to the primary claim. Deploying causal-sensitivity machinery while declaring "hazard ratios are descriptive associations, not causal" (§2.5.4) is having it both ways. **Neutralize:** report the E-value for the baseline-only estimate only, with the caveat, and delete it from §4.7's aggregation.

**6. MAJOR [framing / generalization] — Abstract sentence 1, Keywords, §1, §4.4 vs. §4.9 limitation 1.** Scope creep from "track and field exit" to "youth sport dropout." The outcome counts a sport-switcher as a dropout (§1: "an athlete who leaves track and field for another sport is, from the register's perspective, a dropout"), yet the abstract opens with "Future dropouts from youth sport," and the interpretive sections read the signal through commitment-erosion and role-exit lenses that presuppose psychological withdrawal from sport. Ages 14–16 in multi-sport Norway is precisely the consolidation window; for a plausibly large share of "dropouts," declining track volume is *engagement reallocation* — rising commitment to handball or football — not disengagement, and no analysis bounds this share. This is PSE Referee 1's question 3c(i) in its deep form: the revision fixed the definition (limitation 1) but not the interpretation built on top of it. **Neutralize:** bound the switcher share with external participation data, scope the abstract's first sentence, and add reallocation as a co-equal reading in §4.4.

**7. MAJOR [evidence] — §2.2, §2.5.2, §3.4.** The claims that license the entire construct are uncited, and club-level confounding is unaddressed. The interpretation of volume as "the athlete's own choice" rests on institutional assertions with no citation: open entry, no coach gatekeeping, "entry fees are ordinarily paid by the athlete's club," short travel — §2.2 itself says this "is what licenses interpreting competition volume at ages 13–14 as a behavioral indicator of engagement." But a 13-year-old's meet count is substantially supplied by the club environment (a club's meet-attendance culture, coach activity, training-group vitality), which plausibly also drives retention; controlling club *size* and clustering SEs by club does not control club-level confounding, and the between-club share of volume variance is never reported. This is the deep form of PSE Referee 2's "how much control do youth athletes have?" — answered by assertion, not evidence. **Neutralize:** cite federation regulations/fee policy; report club-level ICC for volume; refit with club fixed or random intercepts.

**8. MAJOR [logic] — §3.3 (Table 3, L3→L4), §3.9, §4.6.** The specialization finding bears the signature of a conditioning artifact plus reverse causation, under a causal heading. HHI is bounded below by 1/n_results, so low-volume athletes are mechanically concentrated; HHI is null alone (OR 1.09, p = .238) and becomes "significant" only after conditioning on volume (OR 1.36) — exactly the pattern conditioning-induced associations produce when predictor and conditioning variable are mechanically coupled. Independently, athletes already intending to continue concentrate on "their" events, making concentration a marker of commitment rather than a protective exposure. The §4.6 hedges are good ("neither a license to push early specialization nor grounds for forced diversification"), but the §3.9 heading "Specialization is protective" uses causal language the paper elsewhere forswears, and practitioners will read the heading. **Neutralize:** recompute HHI restricted to athletes with ≥N results or with a small-sample-corrected index; retitle descriptively.

**9. MAJOR [practical leap] — Abstract, §3.8, §4.8, Table 6.** Prediction is converted to intervention with no evidence of modifiability, and the screen is weak in absolute terms. §4.8 prescribes fee waivers, subsidized travel, and coach-accompanied transport, yet §4.2 concedes the register "cannot fully separate" resource-constrained from motivational decline — so the proposed cost-side interventions target an unidentified subgroup, and reallocation-driven decliners (Issue 6) would not respond at all. The featured threshold (< 10 meets) has sensitivity 0.30 (misses 70% of future dropouts), NPV 0.22 (78% of *unflagged* athletes also drop out), and lift 0.94/0.84 ≈ 1.12: in a population where nearly everyone leaves, the tool separates "very high risk" from "high risk." The abstract's "early-warning window of several years for retention-supporting intervention" presupposes the untested step. **Neutralize:** reframe §4.8 as hypothesis-generating, foreground the §4.10 quasi-experimental proposal as the required next step, and lead with lift and absolute risks rather than PPV.

**10. MAJOR [practical leap / framing] — §4.8(1), §2.7, Statements.** "Register-based behavioral surveillance… fully automatic, with no athlete contact" proposes automated risk-profiling of 13–14-year-old children with zero discussion of consent, transparency, operational GDPR basis (the research exemption in §2.7 does not cover federation profiling of minors), or dual use — the same flag that identifies whom to support identifies whom a resource-constrained club might quietly deprioritize. For a coaching journal whose readers would implement this, the omission is not cosmetic. **Neutralize:** one paragraph on ethical guardrails (transparency to families, support-only use, data-protection basis) and an explicit warning against triage use.

**11. MAJOR [framing / internal consistency] — §4.5 vs. Table 3.** The sex-effect discussion contradicts the paper's own primary model. §4.5 leads with "no clear sex difference in retention (log-rank p = .33)" and a cohort-inconsistent Cox HR, but the primary retention regressions show females at OR 0.72 (sex-only) to 0.61 (full model), p ≤ .012 throughout Table 3 — a 28–39% lower odds of senior retention, significant in every layer and never mentioned in §4.5. Different outcomes (time-to-cessation vs. senior status) may reconcile the two, but the section as written selectively cites the null analysis while a SAGER-compliant paper should confront its own strongest sex result. **Neutralize:** reconcile explicitly in §4.5.

**12. MINOR [evidence / reporting] — §2.4.2, §2.4.4, §2.5.5, §4.2, Abstract.** Declared-but-unreported analyses invite a selective-reporting reading. The random forest "convergent variable-importance evidence" (§2.5.5) appears in no results section or supplement provided; tyrving_slope_13_16, tyrving_mean, tyrving_peak_pre15, res_age_X, and year_round_age_X are defined and never reported; n_champ_types surfaces only in a supplementary figure yet the abstract lists "championship participation" among what was recorded; and the §4.2 periodization rebuttal ("followed by exit… rather than by a performance peak") implies a performance-trajectory analysis that is not shown. **Neutralize:** report, cite to specific supplementary tables, or delete.

**13. MINOR [internal consistency] — Abstract, §3.3, §3.9, §3.10, §4.6, Tables 3–7.** Numbers change between sections. Volume CI: abstract and §3.3 give [2.07, 2.78]; Table 3 gives [2.08, 2.76]. HHI: §3.3/§3.9/§4.6 give OR 1.35 [1.16, 1.57]; Table 3 gives 1.36 [1.17, 1.57]; §4.6 later uses 1.36 in the same paragraph that used 1.35. Table 4 reports n = 1,914 with Tyrving as a covariate although cohort-wide complete-case n is 1,704, unexplained. The outcome-definition sensitivity ORs (2.79, 3.08, 2.94; §3.10) do not include the primary 2.40, so the reader cannot map definitions to estimates. Tables 5 and 7 are cited (§2.5.4, §3.7, §3.10, §4.1 — including the quoted HR = 0.14) but absent from the full-manuscript file (numbering jumps Table 4 → Table 6); possibly a compilation artifact, but as provided these claims are uncheckable. **Neutralize:** full numeric audit and complete table set.

**14. MINOR [framing] — Abstract vs. §3.8/Table 6.** The abstract keeps the numbers and drops the hedges. Results carefully attribute the high PPV "partly" to the 84% base rate (§3.8, Table 6 note); the abstract reports "PPV ≈ 0.94" with no base-rate context. "An early-warning window of several years" measures the gap to the *outcome definition* (age 20+), not to behavioral exit — the median flagged dropout is at zero meets by age 16, so the practical window is one to two seasons. **Neutralize:** add base rate and exit-relative lead time to the abstract.

**15. MINOR [evidence / framing] — §1.2.** The novelty claim survives on a narrow definition. "The trajectory of registered competition behavior… has not, to our knowledge, been examined" is scoped to competition registers, but the manuscript never discusses membership-register and participation-trajectory studies (the tradition its own Eime et al., 2013 citation belongs to), so the reader cannot tell whether the gap is real or definitional — PSE Referee 1's point 5 answered in letter, not in spirit. **Neutralize:** one paragraph positioning competition registers against membership-register trajectory research.

## Ignored Alternative Explanations

- **Engagement reallocation / multi-sport consolidation (Issue 6):** declining track volume as rising commitment to another sport — a *decision*, but not disengagement from sport, and incompatible with the commitment-erosion reading of §4.4.
- **Injury and illness:** a two-season injury produces reduced-then-zero volume and later non-retention with no deliberative process; never mentioned anywhere in the manuscript despite being a leading cause of adolescent athletics attrition.
- **Club and opportunity structure (Issue 7):** volume as supply (club meet culture, coach activity, local meet density), not demand; club size is a weak proxy.
- **Biological maturation:** late maturers at 13–14 experience less success, may enter less, and exit more; birth-quarter controls capture relative age, not maturation status, so volume may partly proxy maturation-linked success feedback.
- **Mechanical life events:** school transition at 16 (including sports-gymnasium relocation), family relocation, coach departure or conflict — all produce the same footprint; §4.4's own closing concession ("the register cannot adjudicate between these mechanisms, but it demonstrates that *some* mechanism left a footprint") admits the set includes non-motivational members, yet the section's weight sits on deliberative withdrawal.
- **Discrete-early decision:** an athlete who decides at 14 to finish one last season produces the "gradual" footprint with no protracted deliberation; the register cannot separate a slow decision from a slow execution of a fast decision.

## Missing Stakeholder Perspectives

- **The athletes (minors):** no consideration of whether being algorithmically flagged as a probable dropout — "with no athlete contact" — is acceptable to the children profiled, or of labeling/self-fulfilling-prophecy risks.
- **Parents and families:** the logistics providers whose constraints partially generate the "behavioral" signal; also the parties who would receive outreach.
- **Volunteer clubs and coaches:** implementation burden of a two-screen surveillance system, and the unexamined incentive to use flags for talent triage rather than support.
- **The federation as data controller:** operational profiling of children needs its own GDPR basis; the paper's ethics apparatus covers only the research use.
- **Norwegian sport as a whole:** a track "dropout" who becomes a committed handball player is a policy success; the deficit framing ("risk," "early warning") pathologizes normal adolescent choice, and the paper never asks whether maximal retention in one sport is even the right objective — a question its own Espedalen (2025) citation raises.

## "So What?" Test

Strip the headline to what the evidence strictly supports: *in Norwegian track and field, registered competition frequency at 13–14 strongly predicts registered senior competition (CV-AUC 0.751); level and year-over-year change carry independent information; divergence is visible before the first selective gate; a count threshold at end of age 14 identifies ~26% of athletes with 94% vs. 84% non-retention; within-sport event concentration does not predict dropout and may accompany retention.* That paper still matters — moderately. It is the first long-horizon, full-population quantification of competition trajectories in this sport; the *timing* result (divergence already at 13–14, steepest exit at 17) genuinely informs when federations must act; and the specialization result usefully complicates blanket diversification advice. It would stand as solid descriptive epidemiology with a screening-feasibility exercise, entirely appropriate for a coaching journal. What does not survive the weakening is the theoretical superstructure — Figure 1, the role-exit and commitment-erosion narrative of §4.4, and the title's construct claim — which is precisely the part the title and abstract sell.

## Observations (Non-Defects)

- **The primary model is genuinely prospective.** The baseline-only design (§2.5.1) removes definitional dependence — an athlete with 5 meets at 13–14 *can* retain at 20 — so the strong tautology charge fails against Table 3; it bites only the milestone-window analyses, which the paper itself demotes (§3.7). This is a real improvement over the design the PSE referees saw.
- **The level-vs-change decomposition (§3.5) is the right analytical idea**; the flaw is contamination of the change measure (Issue 1), not the concept.
- **§4.2 and §4.9 are unusually candid** — the epistemic-asymmetry paragraph and limitation 4 name the paper's deepest weakness voluntarily; the periodization rebuttal (multi-year decline ending in exit is not a taper) is sound in logic even if one supporting claim is unreported (Issue 12).
- **§4.6 correctly answers PSE Referee 2 on specialization:** within-sport concentration is a different construct from multisport sampling, and the r = −.05 check against primary-event performance is a genuinely good control.
- **Referee 2's factual complaints are all fixed:** the Espedalen dissertation title, the Wall & Côté characterization, the enthusiastic/constrained SCM description, the Espedalen & Seippel year, combined-event counting, and category co-occurrence (limitation 6 with the 98% hurdles–sprint statistic).
- **The outcome definition is defensible and sensitivity-checked** (three definitions, S9), the detection-capacity substitute for power analysis (S-M2) is appropriate for population data, and cluster-robust SEs, imputation checks, and the zero-volume exclusion (S11) are competent robustness practice.
- **The KM sex-null vs. survey-literature contrast (§4.5) is a genuinely interesting register-vs-survey discrepancy** — the defect is its selective use (Issue 11), not the observation itself.

## Verdict

One issue is CRITICAL (Issue 1: the title-level within-athlete "pull-back" process claim is supported by no exit-aligned or contamination-free analysis), which blocks editorial Accept; my overall position is that the manuscript contains a publishable and valuable descriptive core, but as framed it asserts a disengagement process its own analyses cannot distinguish from stable typology, abrupt exit, and engagement reallocation, and it must either produce exit-aligned individual-level evidence or retreat to the descriptive claim in title, abstract, and §4.4.

## Closing

This panel returns a decision of **Major Revision** with genuine enthusiasm for the underlying work. Every seat — including the one whose job was to break the paper — affirms that the register design is novel, the population-complete 14-year follow-up is a real contribution to a literature that has structurally lacked behavioral trajectories, and the descriptive core is publishable (EIC-S1, R1-S1, R2-S1, R3-S1, DA "So What?" test). What separates this manuscript from acceptance is not its evidence base but three things the revision can deliver: one decisive new analysis that either earns or retires the title's process claim (M1); a results pipeline whose every number is traceable end-to-end (M2, M6–M9); and a practical-implications section rebuilt so that the coaches and federations who are this journal's readership can act on it safely and honestly (M3–M5). The prior PSE objections have been substantially neutralized — all three peer reviewers confirm this item by item — so the required work is forward-looking, not remedial of old ground.
