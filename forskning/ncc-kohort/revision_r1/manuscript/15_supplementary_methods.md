# Supplementary Methods (Extended)

*This material accompanies the main article and expands the condensed Statistical Analysis section. Citations here appear in the supplementary reference list below.*

## S-M1. Time-to-cessation survival analysis: full specification

We modeled time from baseline to last active season using Cox proportional hazards regression with Efron's tie-handling (Efron, 1977), implemented in lifelines (Davidson-Pilon, 2019). The primary Cox specification (Supplementary Table S16) contains a baseline-only predictor set. We additionally estimated (a) a landmark analysis (van Houwelingen, 2007) restricted to athletes active at age 16 (≥1 registered result; n = 1,167, complete-case n = {+1,153+}; Supplementary Table S8), and (b) a post-baseline Cox specification with ages-15–16 covariates (Table 5 gives the period-specific decomposition; Supplementary Figures S1 and S4 give its calibration and forest plots, and Supplementary Tables S6 and S10 the HHI-stratified and pre-milestone-only variants); the latter is interpreted with caveats about operational overlap between predictor and outcome windows. Two properties of the landmark analysis deserve note. First, the activity criterion at the landmark is ≥1 result (rather than the ≥2 used for active seasons) so that the conditioning event is minimal presence, not the outcome construct itself. Second, follow-up time is measured from age 16, so the age-16 portion of the exposure window (volume at ages 15–16) lies at the very start of the at-risk window; the fully contamination-free version of the same question is therefore the logistic change model of main-text Section 3.5 (level at 15 + change 15→16 among athletes active at 16, outcome at 20+), which involves no overlap at all. Proportional hazards were assessed via scaled Schoenfeld residuals (Grambsch & Therneau, 1994). Pooled hazard ratios from the post-baseline specification average over the strongly time-varying pattern shown in Table 5 and are reported descriptively; no robustness quantity (e.g., E-value) is computed from that specification. Cluster-robust standard errors on club name (Lin & Wei, 1989) and sex-stratified models are reported in Supplementary Tables S2 and S7. Kaplan–Meier curves were stratified by sex, ages-15–16 volume, and pre-17 championship types. {+The event is the final active season observed through 2025 (S-M10).+}

## S-M2. Sample size and detection capacity

Classical hypothesis-testing sample-size power calculations are not directly applicable to population-based register studies where the full cohort is observed; we treat the estimates as descriptions of this population rather than inferences to a hypothetical super-population (Berk & Freedman, 2003), while retaining confidence intervals and p-values as summaries of stochastic (process) variability in who retains. We therefore replace a-priori power with a detection-capacity analysis: given the observed event count, what is the smallest effect size the design could reliably detect, relative to effect sizes in prior dropout research? Following Hsieh & Lavori (2000), our d = {+1,934+} Cox events at α = .05 and 80% power yield HR_min ≈ 1.07 per SD (≈ 1.15 for a balanced binary covariate); the omitted variance-inflation factor for covariate correlation would move this only trivially (to ≈ 1.08 at ρ ≈ .3–.45). For the primary logistic analysis (n = {+2,095, 344+} retainers), simulation (300 replicates per grid point, Wald test) gives a minimum detectable odds ratio of ≈ 1.20 per SD at 80% power. Prior prospective work on youth-sport dropout reports small-to-moderate effects (Cohen's d ≈ 0.2–0.5; ORs ≈ 1.4–2.5; Back et al., 2022; Calvo et al., 2010; Espedalen & Seippel, 2024; Sarrazin et al., 2002). Detection capacity is therefore well below typical effect magnitudes, so null findings should be interpreted as substantively small rather than underpowered.

## S-M3. {+Performance scoring, missing data, multiple imputation, and athletes without registered sex+}

{+*Tyrving scoring.* Every result is scored when its event and implement or hurdle specification match a row of the Tyrving table (Norwegian Athletics Federation, 2014 version) for the athlete's sex and calendar age; the register codes events with their specification (e.g., shot put 3 kg, 60 m hurdles 76.2 cm), and the table gives one row per age-appropriate specification, so all events contested at the baseline meet (sprints, middle distances, hurdles, jumps, throws, race walking) are covered. Take-off-zone long and triple jumps, used in these age groups, have no separate norm and are scored against the board-jump norm (a slight advantage to zone jumpers; scoring them as missing instead leaves 45 rather than 28 athletes without a baseline score and gives volume OR 2.23 [1.96, 2.52], Tyrving OR 1.28 [1.11, 1.48], and CV-AUC 0.746, essentially unchanged). Race-walk and middle-distance times recorded in minutes.seconds format are converted before scoring. With this scoring, 97.9% of baseline-meet results receive a score.+}

{+*Missing data.* Baseline Tyrving is missing for 28 athletes (1.3%): 24 without registered sex (sex-specific norms cannot be applied), 2 without baseline-meet results in the career extract, and 2 whose baseline implements do not match the table row for their registered sex and age (probable registration errors). HHI, volume and the outcome are complete. The primary analysis is therefore complete-case (n = 2,095); included and excluded athletes are compared in Supplementary Table S25, and every analysis sample is mapped in Supplementary Table S12. Multiple imputation used chained equations (van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011) as implemented in scikit-learn's iterative imputer: Bayesian ridge conditional models, draws from the posterior predictive distribution, 15 iterations, and m = 20 imputed datasets with different seeds. The imputation model contains the outcome (active senior status), sex, baseline Tyrving, HHI and pre-milestone volume; a sensitivity analysis adds auxiliary variables (primary baseline event category, region, birth cohort, club size, and the number of results at ages 13–14). Sex is never imputed (the 24 athletes without registered sex are excluded beforehand, n = 2,099). Estimates were pooled with Rubin's rules. Predictive performance under imputation was estimated with the imputation model fitted inside each training fold, without the outcome (so no outcome information reaches the validation fold), and applied to the held-out fold; this was repeated for each of the 20 imputations and the CV-AUCs averaged (Supplementary Table S21).+}

{+Because only four sex-known athletes lack a score under the revised scoring, imputation and complete-case analysis coincide. Supplementary Table S21 therefore also reports the same procedures on the original, incomplete scoring (Tyrving missing for 395 of 2,099 sex-known athletes, 18.8%), where imputation matters: pooled estimates (volume OR 2.31 [2.04, 2.61] with the outcome-including model; 2.30 [2.03, 2.60] with auxiliary variables) and predictive performance (CV-AUC 0.746, range 0.740–0.749 across imputations) were the same as complete-case (2.33 [2.02, 2.68]; CV-AUC 0.746). Mean imputation (Supplementary Table S5) and cluster-robust standard errors at the club level (Supplementary Table S2) give the same picture.+}

{+*Athletes without registered sex.* The register records sex as a binary variable; 24 cohort athletes (1.1%) have none. They are excluded from all regression models, which condition on sex, and from Tyrving scoring, but included in cohort totals and unstratified Kaplan–Meier curves. Their senior retention was 12.5% (3 of 24) and their median pre-milestone volume 16 meets. In a sensitivity model without Tyrving that retains them as a separate sex category (n = 2,123), the volume OR was 2.37 [2.10, 2.67], versus 2.36 [2.09, 2.66] when they are excluded (n = 2,099).+}

## S-M4. {+Cross-validation, calibration, and comparison of discrimination+}

{+All cross-validation uses stratified 5-fold splits repeated 20 times with different seeds; in each training fold the standardization of continuous covariates is re-estimated and applied to the held-out fold (a scikit-learn pipeline), and an unpenalized logistic model is fitted. The CV-AUC is the mean of the 100 fold-level AUCs. Its 95% CI, and the CIs and p-values for differences between models fitted on identical folds, use the corrected resampled t-statistic for repeated k-fold cross-validation, which inflates the variance of the fold-level estimates by n_test/n_train to account for overlapping training sets (Nadeau & Bengio, 2003). Calibration was assessed on the out-of-fold predicted probabilities averaged over the 20 repeats: the calibration slope (logistic regression of the outcome on the linear predictor), calibration-in-the-large (intercept of a logistic model with the linear predictor as offset), and the Brier score. To test transfer across clubs, the cross-validation was repeated with stratified group folds, keeping every baseline club (287 clubs) entirely within either the training or the validation fold.+}

{+Standardizing on the full data before cross-validation, as in the original submission, cannot leak outcome information, because the standardization uses no outcome data and an unpenalized logistic regression is invariant to affine transformations of its covariates; re-running the submitted analysis with standardization inside the folds reproduced its AUC, calibration and Brier score to the third decimal (Supplementary Table S26). For the early-warning thresholds (Table 6) we computed sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive value, and absolute senior-retention rates among flagged and unflagged athletes on a single denominator (the full cohort, n = 2,123) with 2,000-replicate bootstrap percentile confidence intervals.+}

## S-M5. E-values for unmeasured confounding

E-values (VanderWeele & Ding, 2017) are computed for the primary baseline-window estimates only. Because the outcome is common (16.4%), the primary odds ratio is first converted to an approximate risk ratio via RR ≈ √OR before applying E = RR + √(RR(RR − 1)): OR {+2.25 → RR ≈ 1.50 → E ≈ 2.4 (CI bound 2.2)+}. The baseline-only Cox hazard ratio ({+0.54+} per SD) is converted with the common-outcome formula RR ≈ (1 − 0.5^√HR)/(1 − 0.5^√(1/HR)), giving E ≈ {+2.4+}. An earlier version of this analysis computed E-values from the post-baseline (ages-15–16) specification; those values are not reported because that specification both overlaps the outcome window and violates proportional hazards, making a pooled hazard ratio an inappropriate E-value input.

## S-M6. Exit-aligned trajectories, change models, {+and the within-athlete fixed-effects model+}

To test the within-athlete {+decline+} claim at the individual level, each dropout's volume history was aligned to their own final active season (last calendar year with ≥2 results). For dropouts with final seasons at ages 15–19 (n = 1,139; ages outside 13–19 are unobserved in the per-age panel), we report median volumes at T−3, T−2, T−1, and T, the share still competing (>0 meets) in the penultimate season, and, among dropouts with final seasons at 16+ (n = 795, for whom at least one season precedes the penultimate), the share whose penultimate-season volume was reduced but nonzero relative to their earlier personal peak, at their peak (abrupt profile), or zero (gap year). The contamination-free change model is a logistic regression among athletes with ≥2 results at age 16 (n = 1,075): active senior status on sex, volume at age 15 (per SD), and change in volume from 15 to 16 (per SD{+; volume at 16 minus volume at 15+}). All predictors are measured by age 16 and every athlete in the sample is, by construction, still active when measured; the outcome window begins at age 20. {+In both change models, change is defined as the later minus the earlier volume, so an odds ratio above 1 per SD increase is equivalent to an odds ratio below 1 (its reciprocal) per SD of decline; because the models are linear in the logit, a model with the earlier level and the change is a re-parameterization of one with the two levels, and the per-meet odds ratio for change equals that for the later level.+}

{+*Fixed-effects model.* The panel contains, for every cohort athlete, the number of distinct meets in each calendar age-year from 13 to 19 (zero when no result was registered). For athletes whose exit was observed (final active season before 2024), seasons after the final active season are excluded, because they are inactive by definition. The outcome is log(1 + meets); the model includes athlete fixed effects (estimated by the within transformation), age fixed effects, and indicators for the final active season (T) and the three preceding seasons (T−1, T−2, T−3). The reference category comprises all seasons of athletes still active and all seasons more than three seasons before an observed exit, so the indicators measure how each athlete's volume in the seasons before exit deviates from that athlete's own volume in earlier seasons, net of the age profile common to all athletes. Standard errors are clustered by athlete; athletes with a single season contribute nothing to the within estimates (1,897 athletes and 8,447 athlete-seasons remain). Two robustness versions were estimated: the same model with meet counts on the linear scale, and the log model restricted to exits at ages 17–19 (plus athletes still active), for whom seasons more than three years before exit are observed. A Wald test compares the T−1 and T−3 coefficients (Supplementary Table S28; Supplementary Figure S5).+}

## S-M7. Club-level analyses

The intraclass correlation of pre-milestone volume across baseline clubs was estimated from a random-intercept linear mixed model (ICC = between-club variance / total variance). The primary logistic model was refit with club random intercepts via a Bayesian binomial mixed model (variational approximation), Supplementary Table S22{+; club-grouped cross-validation is described in S-M4+}.

## S-M8. HHI {+window,+} count-dependence stress tests{+, and combined events+}

{+The HHI used in all models is computed from results in the age-13 and age-14 calendar years, the same window as the volume predictor. Combined events appear in the register both as a total and as separate constituent results; constituents are counted in their natural categories (multi-event athletes register as diversified; stand-alone combined rows <0.1%).+} Because HHI ≥ 1/n for an athlete with n results, low result counts mechanically inflate apparent concentration. Three checks (Supplementary Table S20): Spearman correlations of HHI with result and meet counts {+at ages 13–14+}; refits of the primary model restricted to athletes with ≥5 and ≥8 results {+at ages 13–14+}; and a refit replacing HHI with the finite-sample-corrected index HHI* = (HHI − 1/n)/(1 − 1/n).

## {+S-M9. Derivation and validation of early-warning thresholds across birth cohorts+}

{+Because the candidate threshold (< 10 meets) was chosen in the pooled data, cut-offs were also derived in one birth cohort and evaluated, unchanged, in the other (Supplementary Table S29). Two pre-specified derivation rules were used: a capacity rule that flags the lowest quartile of pre-milestone volume in the derivation cohort (athletes at or below its 25th percentile), which corresponds to the targeting logic of Section 4.8, and the cut-off maximizing Youden's J (sensitivity + specificity − 1) over 2–40 meets. Performance in the evaluation cohort is reported with 2,000-replicate bootstrap percentile CIs. Both cohorts come from the same register and national system, so this is temporal validation within one setting, not external validation.+}

## {+S-M10. Temporary gaps, returns, and alternative event definitions+}

{+An active season is a calendar year with ≥2 results. Both outcomes use the final active season observed through 2025, so a gap followed by a return is part of a continuing career rather than an exit, and athletes active in 2024 or 2025 are censored (time-to-event) because a final season cannot yet be distinguished from a gap. To quantify how often gaps end in a return, we counted athletes with inactive seasons between baseline and their final active season, and, among athletes who at some point before 2020 missed two consecutive seasons (so that at least four later seasons are observable), the share who ever competed again. The baseline-only Cox model was then re-estimated with two alternative event definitions that do not use later returns: the first sustained exit (the last active season before the first run of two or more inactive seasons) and the first inactive season (the spell ends at the first season without ≥2 results) (Supplementary Table S30).+}

## {+S-M11. The cohort within the register population+}

{+To describe the target population, we extracted from the register (September 2026 version) every athlete born 1998–2002 with at least one result at ages 13–14 (n = 7,327) and computed, from the same register rows for cohort members and non-members alike, the number of distinct meets at ages 13–14 and senior status (≥2 results in a calendar year at age 20 or later). Senior status agreed with the analysis dataset for 99.7% of cohort members (the six discrepancies reflect register updates after April 2026). Because the baseline meet is stored in more than one register row, the raw meet counts for cohort members are on average 2.5 meets higher than the de-duplicated counts used in the main analyses; the comparison of cohort and non-cohort athletes in Supplementary Table S31 is therefore indicative. The sex-adjusted association between volume and senior status was estimated per 10 meets, with repeated cross-validated AUC.+}
