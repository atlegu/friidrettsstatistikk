# Results

## 3.1 Cohort characteristics

The cohort comprised 2,123 athletes (52.0% female): 1,301 in Cohort A and 822 in Cohort B, closely matched at baseline (mean Tyrving {+712 and 715+} and ~41% active at 17 in each; Table 1). Senior retention (≥1 season with ≥2 results at age 20+) was 15.8% in Cohort A and 17.3% in Cohort B (combined 16.4%). The proportion still active in 2024+ was 5.8% (A) and 10.8% (B), reflecting Cohort B's shorter follow-up (fixed-window outcome at ages 20–22: 15.3% vs 16.7%; Supplementary Table S24). {+Of all 7,327 register athletes born 1998–2002 with a result at ages 13–14, the cohort is 29%, but it contains 78% of those with ten or more meets at those ages and 77% of all who later competed as seniors; the others had a median of 2 meets and 2.0% senior retention (Supplementary Table S31).+}

## 3.2 Competition-volume trajectories diverge by ages 13–14

Figure 2 presents the central behavioral observation (Figure 1 shows the conceptual model): median competitions per year by age, plotted separately for eventual retainers (n = 348) and non-retainers (n = 1,775). The groups already differ at baseline (medians of 13 vs. 8 meets at age 13, and 17 vs. 8 at age 14), and from age 15 they diverge sharply: retainers peak at 19 meets and sustain that level through 17, while future dropouts fall from 8 to 3 to 0. Baseline differences and within-athlete {+decline+} are separated formally in Section 3.5.

Kaplan–Meier curves summarize the attrition (medians in Table 2): half the cohort ceased active competition within 3 years, 75% within 5, steepest at age 17, the youth-to-junior transition (Supplementary Figure S2); sex-stratified curves overlap (log-rank *p* = .33). {+Descriptively, curves by ages-15–16 volume separate monotonically (71% senior retention with ≥31 meets vs. 4% with none; Figure 3), as do curves by pre-17 championship types (Supplementary Figure S3).+}

## 3.3 Primary analysis: prospective logistic regression for senior retention

**Among baseline-window predictors (ages 13–14), competition volume provided the largest incremental contribution to prospective discrimination of senior retention 6–14 years later: OR = {+2.25+} per SD (95% CI {+[1.99, 2.55]+}, *p* < .001), cross-validated AUC = {+0.746 (95% CI 0.716–0.776)+}.** {+In the stepwise models (Table 3), CV-AUC was 0.535 (sex), 0.641 (+ Tyrving), 0.636 (+ HHI), and 0.746 (+ volume; increase 0.110, 95% CI 0.081–0.140; Supplementary Table S27)+}, with well-calibrated predictions (calibration slope {+0.98, calibration-in-the-large 0.00, Brier 0.120+}; Supplementary Table S23). {+In the full model, baseline performance (OR = 1.23 per SD, 95% CI [1.06, 1.42]) and HHI (OR = 1.27 per SD, 95% CI [1.12, 1.44]) retained smaller associations (§3.9).+}

## 3.4 Structural and club-level controls do not change the picture

Adding region, birth quarter (Q1/Q4), and club size left the volume coefficient {+essentially unchanged (OR = 2.26 vs. 2.25)+}; no structural covariate was significant (*p* > {+.17+}; Supplementary Table S13{+; full quarter coding: χ²(3) = 3.24, p = .36+}). A quarter of volume variance lies between clubs (ICC = {+0.24, 287+} clubs), but with club random intercepts the coefficient was, if anything, larger (OR = {+2.43+}, 95% CI {+[2.17, 2.72]+}; Supplementary Table S22){+, and folds grouped by club gave the same cross-validated AUC (0.746 [0.709, 0.783]; Supplementary Table S26)+}: the association holds within clubs {+and carries over to clubs not used for fitting+}.

## 3.5 {+Within-athlete decline+} versus baseline heterogeneity

**Early behavioral heterogeneity and within-athlete {+decline+} contribute independently; they are not substitutes.** {+Four+} analyses separate the two readings of Figure 2.

First, level and change. Among athletes still active at age 14 (1,914; complete-case model n = {+1,888+}), the age-14 volume level (OR = {+2.70+} per SD, 95% CI {+[2.33, 3.12]+}) and the change from age 14 to 15 (OR = {+2.38 per SD increase+}, 95% CI {+[2.08, 2.73]; i.e., OR = 0.42 per SD, about 6.5 meets, of decline+}) contributed independently (both *p* < .001); adding change {+raised pseudo-*R*² from 0.122 to 0.229+} (Table 4).

Second, because a change score spanning the exit could conflate {+decline+} with the exit itself, we repeated the test where no exit can contaminate it: among athletes still competing at age 16 (≥2 results; n = 1,075), volume at 15 (OR = 3.04, 95% CI [2.55, 3.62]) and change from 15 to 16 (OR = 1.88 {+per SD increase+}, 95% CI [1.60, 2.20]{+; 0.53 per SD of decline+}; both *p* < .001) predicted senior status four or more years later (CV-AUC = {+0.761+}): decline predicts exit among athletes who are all, at measurement, still active.

Third, exit-aligned trajectories. Aligned to each dropout's own final active season (n = 1,139 with final seasons at ages 15–19), median volumes at T−3, T−2, T−1, and T were 12, 10, 9, and 4 meets{+; 95% were still competing in their penultimate season, and among final seasons at 16+ (n = 795), 74% had a *reduced-but-nonzero* penultimate season relative to their earlier peak, 6.5% a gap year, and 19% exited from peak volume (Supplementary Table S19)+}.

{+Fourth, the athlete fixed-effects model (1,897 athletes; Supplementary Table S28, Supplementary Figure S5) placed volume 7% (95% CI 0–13%), 12% (5–19%), and 24% (16–31%) below each athlete's own earlier level three, two, and one season(s) before the final active season, net of the common age profile; the decline steepened towards exit (*p* < .001) and was reproduced among exits at ages 17–19.+}

## 3.6 Time-aligned behavior vs. performance

In a fully time-aligned comparison, with behavior and performance both measured at ages 13–14 to remove any temporal advantage, {+volume discriminated better: CV-AUC = 0.641 for sex + baseline Tyrving and 0.739 for sex + volume (difference 0.098, 95% CI 0.063–0.133), and adding Tyrving to volume changed nothing (difference −0.002 [−0.011, 0.007]; Supplementary Table S27). A within-baseline performance trajectory (Tyrving change from 13 to 14) raised performance-based discrimination to 0.701 (n = 1,692), narrowing but not reversing the gap (volume 0.734; difference 0.033 [−0.003, 0.069]).+}

Volume and performance were {+moderately+} correlated (Spearman ρ = {+.38+} with baseline Tyrving; {+.55+} with pre-15 peak; {+.60+} for milestone volume vs. age-15 Tyrving), {+but above-median volume was associated with higher senior retention within every baseline-Tyrving quartile, from 15.4% vs. 9.5% in the lowest to 36.0% vs. 13.9% in the highest (Supplementary Table S32)+}. Volume is related to, but clearly not reducible to, performance level.

Conditioning on athletes active at age 16 (≥1 result; n = 1,167), volume across ages 15–16 was the largest contributor to subsequent cessation risk (HR = {+0.60+} per SD, 95% CI {+[0.55, 0.66]+}, C-index = 0.735; complete-case n = {+1,153+}; Supplementary Table S8); excluding athletes with milestone volume = 0 gave HR = {+0.52+} (Supplementary Table S11). {+The baseline-only Cox model (Supplementary Table S16), a period-specific decomposition (Table 5), and diagnostics (Supplementary Tables S1, S6, S10, S17; Supplementary Figures S1, S4) are descriptive companions; post-baseline Cox estimates are not primary (predictor and at-risk window overlap), and their contamination-free analogue is the change model in Section 3.5.+}

## 3.8 Prospective early-warning thresholds

Because the primary analysis uses only baseline-window data, a prospective rule can be evaluated at the end of the age-14 season (Table 6, with bootstrap CIs). At a moderate {+candidate+} threshold (vol < 10 meets across ages 13–14, flagging 26.4%), senior retention was 5.7% among flagged athletes versus 20.2% among unflagged, a 3.5-fold difference in retention prospects (PPV = 0.94, sensitivity = 0.30); lower (< 5) and higher (< 15) thresholds narrow or broaden the screen. Two properties discipline use: PPV partly reflects the 84% base rate (~10-point precision gain), and NPV = 0.20 means unflagged athletes are *not* "safe": four in five also fail to retain. {+Derived in one cohort and tested in the other, the rule held: the lowest volume quartile in Cohort A is exactly < 10 meets, which in Cohort B flagged 24.8%, with retention 4.9% versus 21.4% (PPV = 0.95), and the reverse direction was similar (Supplementary Table S29). Youden's J favours broader screens (< 21 to < 29 meets), so < 10 remains a candidate targeting rule.+}

## 3.9 Within-sport event concentration and retention

Higher baseline HHI (more event-category concentration {+at ages 13–14+}) {+was associated with+} higher senior retention: OR = {+1.27+} per SD (95% CI {+[1.12, 1.44]+}, *p* < .001) in the primary regression {+and HR = 0.89 [0.85, 0.94] in the baseline-only Cox model, but the association was modest: clear in Cohort B (OR = 1.44), not significant in Cohort A (OR = 1.16, 95% CI [0.97, 1.37]), and without gain in discrimination (ΔAUC = −0.005 [−0.017, 0.008])+}. Because HHI is bounded below by 1/(result count), we stress-tested for count-dependence; the association {+was unchanged under+} restriction to ≥5 and ≥8 {+results at ages 13–14 (OR = 1.26, 1.24)+} and a finite-sample-corrected index (OR = {+1.29+}, 95% CI {+[1.14, 1.46]+}; Supplementary Table S20){+: it is not a small-count artifact. The index captures concentration *within* track and field, not multisport diversification (§4.6).+}

## 3.10 {+Internal cohort replication+} and sensitivity

Re-estimated by cohort, the pre-milestone volume effect {+was reproduced+}: Cohort A OR = {+2.22+} (95% CI {+[1.89, 2.60]+}), Cohort B OR = {+2.33+} (95% CI {+[1.90, 2.87]+}), both *p* < .001 (Table 7); {+female sex predicted lower retention in both cohorts+}.
