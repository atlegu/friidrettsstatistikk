# Discussion

## 4.1 Summary of findings

Four findings emerge from this 14-year follow-up of {+2,138+} young athletes. First, competition volume at ages 13–14 was strongly and prospectively associated with retaining senior competition{+, more strongly than performance at the baseline meet, which nonetheless added complementary information+}. Second, the association {+was reproduced in both birth cohorts+} and withstood structural controls, club random effects, {+club-clustered and club-grouped validation,+} and multiple imputation. Third, the dropout signal {+had+} two {+components+}: early behavioral heterogeneity visible at 13–14, and a {+within-athlete decline in competition participation before exit+}. {+Dropouts' participation typically declined+} (median 12 → 10 → 9 → 4 meets across their last four seasons), {+falling below their own earlier level before exit,+} and decline predicted exit even among athletes all still active at 16. Fourth, {+event-category concentration within track and field was not robustly associated with retention once performance in the athlete's main event was taken into account+}.

## 4.2 {+Competition volume and performance carry complementary information+}

{+Competition volume discriminated senior retention somewhat better than performance (Tyrving score) at the baseline meet (AUC difference 0.040, 95% CI 0.006–0.074), but the two were complementary, and with its within-baseline trajectory performance did not differ from volume (Section 3.6). Hypothesis 2 is thus partially supported: behavior discriminates at least as well as performance, not instead of it.+} Performance reflects capability; volume reflects the athlete's own choices about taking part, and those choices {+carry at least as much information about later retention as+} capability does{+, and partly different information+}. Two caveats: volume is a near-noiseless count {+over two seasons+} while Tyrving is a scored maximum {+from a single meet, with norms that are not equally demanding across event groups+}, so part of the gap could be measurement precision ({+a within-event rank did not close it, and+} the within-stratum effects argue not all of it); and predictor and outcome both index registered activity, so {+volume's advantage+} describes when disengagement becomes observable, not a causal mechanism.

Low or declining volume can also have causes unrelated to waning engagement{+: cost, cited more often by youth from less-resourced families (Espedalen & Seippel, 2024) but attenuated here (clubs pay entry fees, travel is short); injury, the most frequently mentioned dropout reason in the 25-year Norwegian track-and-field follow-up (24.3%; Enoksen, 2011); a move to another sport (§4.4); or school constraints. All leave the same register signature, hence we call declining participation a marker of disengagement, not a measure of it.+} *Periodization*, competing less in order to peak later, is unlikely: the decline spans successive calendar years and ends in exit, not a peak.

## 4.3 {+Decline is visible within athletes+}, and so is early heterogeneity

Is the divergence in Figure 2 {+a within-athlete decline+}, or early differentiation only, or an artifact of averaging abrupt exits at heterogeneous ages? The exit-aligned {+and fixed-effects+} analyses answer at the individual level: 95% of dropouts were still competing in their penultimate season, only {+20%+} exited directly from peak volume{+, and volume fell below each athlete's own earlier level in the two seasons before exit. The typical exit is preceded by declining participation, not a cliff, although the register shows the timing of this decline, not its reasons.+} Both components are nonetheless {+present+}: {+early level and later change both predicted retention, also among athletes still active at 16 (Section 3.5)+}, and future dropouts already differed at 13–14, consistent with retrospective evidence that dropouts' developmental activities differ years before exit (Wall & Côté, 2007).

## 4.4 Consistent with process accounts of withdrawal

We assess behavior, not motivational processes, so no single framework is confirmed; but the footprint is consistent with the main process accounts. {+In the Sport Commitment Model (Scanlan et al., 1993, 2016), *enthusiastic* commitment is distinguished from *constrained* commitment, the latter less sustainable and linked to burnout (Raedeke, 1997); declining volume before exit fits an eroding commitment balance, though the register cannot distinguish waning enthusiasm from accumulating constraint.+} Withdrawal-as-process accounts (Ebaugh, 1988; Eliasson & Johansson, 2021) describe leaving as a gradual deliberative passage among athletes who remain formally involved: precisely what the exit-aligned trajectories show, a typical future dropout still competing, at reduced volume, for {+one or two+} seasons before the final exit. The register cannot observe the deliberation, but it can now observe its behavioral shape, {+thinning participation before exit+}, at the individual level.

Two readings of the same footprint deserve equal standing{+: disengagement from sport (commitment erosion), or engagement reallocation, since ages 14–16 are when Norwegian multi-sport youth consolidate into one sport, the developmentally appropriate transitions that Battaglia et al. (2024) argue the dropout-as-crisis framing wrongly pathologizes+}. The register cannot separate {+them; Norwegian research is compatible with both, since involvement is strongly stratified (Bakken, 2019; Espedalen, 2025) and exit from organized sport can reclaim time and autonomy rather than mark a loss (Espedalen, 2025)+}. What the register {+shows+} is that *some* mechanism (erosion, reallocation, or constraint) leaves a footprint {+in+} registered activity {+before+} exit.

## 4.5 Sex effect

Kaplan–Meier curves {+of time to the last active season+} showed no clear sex difference (log-rank *p* = {+.42+}), {+but+} females retained {+senior status+} at significantly lower odds in every layer of the primary model (OR = {+0.75+} sex-only, {+0.56+} full model; Table 3) {+and in both cohorts+}: *conditional on the same early volume, concentration, and performance*, girls were less likely to remain, their higher early volumes partially masking the gap. The {+volume association+} itself was similar across sexes (milestone-window volume HR = {+0.48+} for {+both+} males and females; Supplementary Table S7), so the residual sex {+difference lies in the level of retention, not in how volume relates to it+}. This complements survey reports of higher female dropout (Bakken, 2019) and the clearly higher female attrition in Enoksen's (2011) historical cohort.

## 4.6 Within-sport event concentration is a different construct from multisport diversification

Event-category concentration at ages 13–14 {+was associated with greater senior retention only conditional on volume, more clearly in the younger cohort, without discriminative value, and not once performance in the athlete's main event was controlled (Section 3.9): concentrated athletes tend to be those who do well in their main event, and concentration itself adds little. Neither the association nor its absence speaks to the diversification literature+}, which concerns diversification *across sports*: sampling versus single-sport specialization and its consequences for elite performance and health (Côté & Hancock, 2016; Güllich et al., 2022). Our HHI measures concentration *within* one sport, {+and+} an athlete concentrated within track and field may simultaneously be a multisport sampler. {+The across-sport evidence is in any case less settled than often assumed (Larson et al., 2019; Baker et al., 2021).+}

## 4.7 What registers bring

{+Registers address two structural problems in dropout research: withdrawn athletes are hard to recruit, and retrospective accounts compress a continuous process into a remembered decision. They bring no new theory, but a longitudinal behavioral test of theories built largely on cross-sectional or short-prospective evidence: here a well-calibrated baseline-only signal (AUC = 0.767, unchanged under club-grouped validation), an internal cohort replication, and an E-value of ≈2.2.+}

## 4.8 Implications for practitioners

The practical deliverable is a *testable targeting rule*, not a finished intervention: the register can say precisely *whom* retention support would reach and how early; whether such support works is the pilot question in §4.10.

**(1) Register-based flagging at the end of the age-14 season.** Flag athletes whose ages-13–14 competition count falls below a {+candidate+} threshold. At < 10 meets, the flag captures {+27%+} of the cohort whose senior retention is {+5.5%+}, versus {+20.3%+} among the unflagged, a {+3.7-fold+} difference computed automatically from data the federation already holds (Table 6{+; similar when derived in one birth cohort and applied to the other, Supplementary Table S29+}). Every flagged athlete {+has competed (cohort entry guarantees a baseline meet), though 29% had no result at 14,+} so the flag list is an outreach list. {+Sensitivity is 0.31, so most future dropouts are *not* flagged:+} a flag concentrates risk; its absence reassures no one, so flagging should *prioritize* outreach, not substitute for population-wide provision.

**(2) Flag again after age 15.** {+A decline shows as lower later volume (Section 3.5): screen at the end of age 14 and again at the end of age 15,+} the latter just after the first selective gate and ahead of the {+junior years, when the annual exit rate peaks+}.

**(3) Lower the cost side of the next competition cycle, as an offer and never as pressure.** {+The+} remaining levers are residual and psychological costs: membership and equipment support, {+transport+}, low-stakes practice meets, and an explicit signal that participation is welcome regardless of performance (Scanlan et al., 1993, 2016). The register cannot distinguish athletes who are blocked from {+those+} choosing other priorities, for whom retention pressure would be misplaced; understanding *why* {+requires conversation, supported by+} commitment instruments such as the SCQ-2.

**(4) Be cautious about prescribing event diversification for retention.** Within-sport concentration {+was not associated with lower+} retention (§4.6); federations should not assume diversification pressure aids retention in this age group.

**Governance preconditions.** Operational flagging means algorithmic risk-profiling of minors, {+which+} the research-ethics exemption of Section 2.7 {+does not cover+}. Deployment would require a federation's own legal basis and a data-protection impact assessment; flags visible to a designated officer rather than routinely to coaches; informed families with an opt-out; a retention limit for flag data; and attention to equity{+. The flag must be support-only:+} the same list that identifies whom to help could identify whom a stretched club quietly deprioritizes. These preconditions, and the untested intervention effect, are why the next step is {+a pilot (§4.10)+} rather than adoption.

## 4.9 Limitations

First, the outcome indexes *attrition from track-and-field competition*, not from sport: a sport-switcher counts as a dropout, and the reallocation share is unbounded (§4.4). Second, the register records official results, not subjective experience, nor injury, a leading cause of attrition in this sport (Enoksen, 2011); part of the {+decline+} is therefore plausibly non-volitional, and the qualitative literature remains essential for *why* volume declines. Third, {+the target population is the engaged core, athletes entered for the regional 13–14 meet, not all young athletes; among casual participants outside the cohort, volume was also associated with retention (OR = 2.17 per 10 meets; Supplementary Table S31), but the thresholds and absolute rates apply to the engaged core. The+} three venues cover eastern, western, and mid Norway, with northern districts served by the mid-Norway venue. Fourth, predictor and outcome share a measurement scale; despite the baseline-only model, {+the change models, the exit-aligned and fixed-effects analyses, and the landmark analysis+}, structural overlap remains a conceptual limitation of register-only designs. Fifth, the post-baseline Cox specification violated proportional hazards (Schoenfeld χ² = {+263.1+}); its estimates are descriptive only. Sixth, {+Tyrving norms are not equally demanding across event groups (throws and race walking score lower), and+} the HHI treats event categories symmetrically although they co-occur unevenly (98% of hurdlers also sprinted), so identical values can represent different combination patterns. Seventh, {+athletes born in 2002 have four senior seasons of observation (2022–2025)+}, though the fixed-window outcome gives near-identical results. Eighth, the thresholds are {+candidates, checked across two cohorts of one register but not externally validated, and+} calibrated to a complete-register, open-entry, club-pays system{+;+} they do not transfer to coach-gated or pay-to-play systems without re-derivation, and no intervention effect has been demonstrated: §4.8 is targeting logic, not evaluated policy.

## 4.10 Future directions

The central next step is a quasi-experimental pilot: federation outreach to athletes with low pre-milestone volume {+(< 10 meets at ages 13–14)+}, a designated flag recipient per club, pre-registered outcomes, and stakeholder consultation (athletes' councils, parents, club volunteers). Further extensions: linking the behavioral signal to commitment instruments in active athletes, and cross-sport generalization where registers are less complete.

## 4.11 Conclusion

Senior retention in Norwegian youth track and field is prospectively associated with baseline-window competition behavior: athletes who already compete more at ages 13–14 retain at substantially higher rates, and those who decline retain at substantially lower rates. The decline is visible within athletes {+in the seasons+} before exit, and is no averaging artifact. The effect withstands structural, club-level, missing-data, and outcome-definition checks and {+is reproduced in both birth cohorts+}; early heterogeneity and {+within-athlete decline both carry information, and baseline performance adds complementary information+}. The findings are consistent with, though do not by themselves confirm, accounts of withdrawal as a gradual deliberative process, and they yield a register-implementable targeting rule whose intervention value is now a testable question.
