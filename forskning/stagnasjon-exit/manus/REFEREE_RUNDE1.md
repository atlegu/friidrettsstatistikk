# Simulated peer review, round 1 — "Stagnation before exit: performance progression and retention in youth track and field"

*Journal: Sports Medicine – Open. Manuscript: main.pdf (23 pp., numbered lines 1–428). Three independent simulated referees with different specialties; comments refer to the manuscript's line numbers.*

---

## Reviewer 1 — Sport psychologist (motivation, commitment, and dropout in youth sport)

### Summary

The manuscript tests, in a national competition registry, whether within-athlete performance progression at ages 13–16 predicts remaining active as a senior, and whether that association is stronger among relatively younger athletes, as a "mastery-experience" account from a companion paper predicts. The behavioural question is important, the design is honest about what a registry can and cannot show, and the three-mechanism discussion (Section 4.3) is better than most registry papers manage. My main concerns are that the "mastery" construct is theoretically thin and almost entirely self-referential, that the chosen moderator (birth quarter) is a weak operationalisation of the proposed mechanism, and that the practical-implications section outruns what a per-SD odds ratio on group data licenses at the level of an individual athlete.

### Major comments

1. **The "mastery-experience" framework is under-theorised and self-referential (lines 17–19, 76–81, 283–296).** "Mastery experience" is a construct with a specific home in self-efficacy theory (Bandura's sources of efficacy beliefs), and visible personal progress also maps directly onto perceived competence in self-determination theory and onto task/mastery orientation in achievement goal theory. The manuscript instead treats the construct as defined by the author's own companion registry paper [12], and the theoretical base of the paper is effectively three self-citations ([10–12]) out of 18 references. This matters for interpretation: if "mastery" is meant in the self-efficacy sense, the account predicts effects of *perceived* progress, which raises the question of whether age-graded points are visible/salient to 13–16-year-olds at all (do athletes see Tyrving points, or raw times and placings?). The discussion would benefit from (a) anchoring the mastery account explicitly in the established motivation literature (self-efficacy, perceived competence/SDT, achievement goal theory), (b) one or two sentences on what athletes actually observe in this system, and (c) a clearer statement of what the mastery account predicts that a generic "commitment" account does not.

2. **Birth quarter is a weak proxy for the proposed moderator, and the strong conclusion "that prediction failed" (lines 288–293) should be qualified accordingly.** The mechanism in [12], as restated at lines 288–290, is that personal progress matters most for athletes who "lack rank-based reinforcement". Birth quarter is only a coarse, noisy proxy for rank deprivation: many Q1-born athletes are low-ranked and many Q4-born athletes win. The registry contains actual competitive outcomes, so a much more direct test is available: does the progression–retention association vary with the athlete's *rank or performance percentile* within sex × event × age class at 13–14? A null progression × birth-quarter interaction (Table 3) with a direct moderator untested does not justify the categorical "That prediction failed" (line 290) as a verdict on the mechanism — only on its birth-quarter formulation. Either add the rank-based moderation analysis or systematically weaken the mechanism language in the abstract (lines 42–44), Key Points (lines 57–59), Section 4.3, and Conclusions (lines 356–359).

3. **The claim that "progression predicts retention at every level of baseline performance" (lines 286–287) is not supported by any reported analysis.** No progression × level interaction or level-stratified estimates appear in the Results. Table 2 shows adjustment for level, which is not the same thing. Either report the stratified/interaction analysis (which would also interest Reviewer 3's talent-development readership) or delete the claim.

4. **The practical implications (Section 4.5, lines 313–328) need recalibration to the individual level.** The statement that a coach who notices an athlete has stopped improving "has noticed something that doubles the odds of departure" (lines 321–323) translates a per-SD odds ratio estimated on group data into an individual-level heuristic without giving the reader the scale: nowhere is the SD of progression reported in points, so neither coaches nor researchers can map "1 SD" onto Table 1's medians and IQRs. Moreover, the cleanest pre-exit signal at the individual level is a median −7.0 points at T−1 (lines 228–229) on an ~800–1000-point scale with wide dispersion — likely indistinguishable from normal fluctuation for a single athlete. Please (a) report the SD of Δ (and of level) in points, (b) add a sentence on individual-level signal-to-noise, and (c) keep the welcome caution of lines 319–321 (no automated flags) consistent throughout — the "doubles the odds" sentence currently works against it. Finally, a sentence acknowledging that dropout from one sport is often normative sampling behaviour in adolescence (developmental model of sport participation; the Fraser-Thomas et al. perspective already cited at [18]) would guard against a deficit framing of leavers.

5. **The sex gap deserves one more analysis, not just a paragraph (Section 4.4, lines 306–312).** Women leave more at the same level, volume, and progression (OR 0.59; HR 1.19). Given the paper's own emphasis on this as "the most consequential unexplained pattern in this registry" (lines 311–312), please report whether the progression–retention association itself differs by sex (progression × sex interaction or sex-stratified ORs). If visible progress retains boys but not girls, the practical implications of Section 4.5 are sex-specific, and the framing of H1 as "general" needs that nuance.

### Minor comments

- Line 47: "relative age effect" as a keyword is defensible but slightly misleading for a paper whose RAE finding is a null interaction; consider "relative age" or dropping it.
- Lines 61–63: enjoyment is introduced as the dominant prospective predictor; one connecting sentence on how stagnating progress plausibly erodes enjoyment/perceived competence would tie the opening to the mechanism section instead of leaving them parallel.
- Lines 297–305: the three-mechanism paragraph is excellent; consider moving the key sentence ("the registry adds not the mechanism but the observability", lines 303–305) into the Conclusions, where it is the paper's most defensible claim.
- Terminology: "dropout" is used for athletes who fail a competition-based senior-activity criterion (lines 134–136); some will still train or compete unsanctioned. "Disengagement from sanctioned competition" (as at line 45) is the accurate term; a sentence in Methods acknowledging this would help.

### Recommendation

**Major revision.** The behavioural finding looks solid and the limitations are honestly stated, but the theoretical framing, the moderator logic, and the practitioner-facing claims all need work before the mechanism-level conclusions are defensible.

## Reviewer 2 — Epidemiologist / biostatistician (registry and cohort methods)

### Summary

This is a retrospective landmark cohort built from a complete national competition registry, with a clearly described exposure, pre-committed analysis plan, explicit conditioning, a reparametrisation stated openly (lines 150–155), cross-validated discrimination, calibration, clustering checks, and E-values. The statistical craftsmanship is well above average for this literature, and I verified that most headline numbers are consistent across abstract, text, tables, and figures. I nonetheless found one arithmetic inconsistency in the sample flow, one presentation practice (repeated-CV percentile intervals formatted as 95 % intervals) that overstates precision, a headline comparison ("as strong as competition volume") that is contradicted by the manuscript's own Table 2, and several reproducibility gaps (E-value conversion, STROBE materials).

### Major comments

1. **Sample-flow arithmetic error (lines 112–116).** The primary sample is n = 3 808, and "Forty-three athletes lacked an exact birth date and were excluded from the birth-quarter analysis only (H2 n = 3 781)". But 3 808 − 43 = 3 765, not 3 781; conversely, H2 n = 3 781 implies 27 excluded. One of the two numbers is wrong (or the 43 refers to a different denominator, e.g. the 4 910 landmark athletes, in which case the sentence is misleading). Please correct, and add (a) a STROBE flow diagram covering 32 447 → 12 938 → 4 910 → 4 909 → 4 172 → 3 808, with the H2 and exit-aligned subsamples (n = 3 781/3 765?, 1 497, 1 011) branching off, and (b) a completed STROBE checklist as supplementary material — STROBE is claimed at line 102 but neither item is provided.

2. **Repeated-cross-validation percentile intervals are presented as if they were confidence intervals (lines 147–150; Table 2, "ΔAUC M2 − M1 (95 % interval) 0.009 (0.008–0.010)"; Table 3, AUC rows and "Difference −0.001 (−0.002 to −0.001)").** Percentile intervals over 20 CV repetitions quantify Monte Carlo variability of the resampling procedure on one fixed dataset; they do not quantify sampling uncertainty, and their extreme narrowness here (±0.001) will be read as implausibly high precision. This matters most for H3, where "progression discriminated no better than level" (lines 219–223) is an inferential claim resting on an interval of the wrong kind. Please either (a) provide bootstrap-based confidence intervals for the AUC differences (resampling athletes, re-running the CV pipeline), or (b) relabel these intervals explicitly as "variability across CV repetitions" in Methods, both tables, and anywhere the ±0.001 precision is implied, and base the H3 conclusion on an interval that reflects sampling error.

3. **The headline claim that progression predicts retention "as strongly as competition volume" (abstract lines 40–42; Key Points lines 50–53; lines 255–258; Conclusions lines 353–355) is contradicted by the manuscript's own models.** In M2, volume at 15–16 has OR 3.15 per SD (2.54–3.92) versus 2.02 (1.73–2.37) for progression — non-overlapping CIs in the same model — and in the Cox model the contrast is HR 0.47 versus 0.86 per SD. If the intended comparison is with the *companion study's* coefficient for volume at 13–14 ([10], line 257), then say so explicitly and give that number so the reader can check; as written, "the same magnitude as competition volume" invites comparison with Table 2 and fails it. Alternatives: soften to "of the same order as the volume signal documented previously", or formally compare the two standardised coefficients in one model with a CI for their difference. This affects the abstract, Key Points, 4.1, and Conclusions, and in my view is the single most important wording fix in the paper.

4. **The within-baseline specification check is weaker evidence than claimed (lines 168–170, 248–250; Table 4, n = 2 735).** The null OR 1.00 for progression within ages 13→14 is offered as evidence that the main estimate "is not an artefact of result counts or of regression mechanics" (lines 248–250). But the 13→14 change is (a) a one-season change, mechanically noisier than the two-window contrast of two-season aggregates; (b) estimated in the selected subsample with scored results in both single seasons (n = 2 735, 72 % of primary); and (c) attenuated toward null by measurement error regardless of signal. A perfectly real short-horizon signal could produce OR ≈ 1 here. Please either quantify the reliability of the one-year versus cross-window change scores (so the reader can judge expected attenuation) or downgrade the language from "indicating" to "consistent with".

5. **The E-value is not reproducible from the reported information (lines 170–171, 250–252; Table 4).** I could reproduce 2.84 (2.45 for the CI bound) only by first converting OR 2.02 to an RR using the outcome prevalence (~17 %; RR ≈ 1.72) and then applying the VanderWeele–Ding formula; the more common square-root approximation for a non-rare outcome gives ≈ 2.20 (1.96). The conversion method must be stated in Methods for the number to be checkable. Also specify the scale of the statement "roughly triple both the odds ..." (line 251) — the E-value is on the risk-ratio scale.

6. **Differential outcome opportunity across cohorts, and handling of the truncated 2026 season (lines 100–101, 112–114, 134–138, 157–158).** The primary outcome ("two approved results in the same calendar year at age ≥20, with follow-up to the extract date") gives the 1998 cohort up to seven senior seasons but the 2005 cohort essentially two, with the 2026 season truncated at 29 October. The logistic primary analysis ignores this censoring structure; the fixed-window outcome (OR 2.07) and the Cox model mitigate it, and cohort enters as a covariate, but the manuscript should (a) justify why the opportunity-unequal definition is primary rather than the fixed-window version, (b) state explicitly whether 2026 counts as a full season for the youngest cohort's outcome, and (c) confirm that the near-identical cohort-half estimates (2.00/2.03) hold for the fixed-window outcome too.

### Minor comments

- Line 111 says a sensitivity analysis "recovers" the 734 athletes excluded for unscorable results, but the corresponding row (Table 4, "best event group at 15–16, switchers included") has n = 3 714 < 3 808. Explain the flow: how many of the 734 re-enter, how many primary-sample athletes drop out of that definition, and why the net n falls.
- Lines 155–156: confirm the H2 model includes the birth-quarter main effect (linear trend) alongside the Δ × quarter interaction, and consider reporting the Q4-vs-Q1 interaction contrast in addition to the linear trend.
- Table 3: no n is given for the H2 rows (3 781 or 3 765, pending comment 1) or the H3 rows; add.
- Lines 130–132: report the SDs used for standardisation (progression, level, volumes) in points/days — needed to interpret "per SD" and for any practical translation.
- Δ and level enter linearly; Figure 2 suggests monotonicity, but a spline sensitivity (or a statement that quintile dummies gave the same picture) would be cheap reassurance.
- Lines 196–199: interpreting covariate coefficients in M2 (volume 3.15 "remained the strongest predictor", female 0.59) flirts with the Table-2 fallacy; the sex coefficient especially should be flagged as a conditional association, as done more carefully in 4.4.
- Figure 2 caption: tertile bounds "≤5, 5–19, ≥19" are ambiguous at the boundaries (5 and 19 appear in two intervals); state half-open intervals.
- Figure 4 versus line 237: the Cox runs "from age 17" but the KM x-axis starts at 16 with visible drops before 17; define the time origin and risk set at t0.
- Lines 225–231: the exit-aligned performance analysis conditions on competing in the main event group in all four final seasons (n = 1 011 of 3 160 dropouts); state this selection and its direction (persistent competitors) explicitly in the figure caption as well.
- Line 172–173: "H2 was directional" — but a two-sided CI is reported and p = 0.40 appears two-sided; clarify what "directional" implied operationally (one-sided α? prediction of sign only?).
- Commendation, with a request: the version-controlled pre-committed analysis plan (lines 93–96, 348–351) is a genuine strength; please deposit it (with commit hash and date) as supplementary material so reviewers and readers can verify what was fixed in advance.

### Recommendation

**Major revision.** The design and transparency are strong and nothing I found threatens the central association, but the flow-count error, the CV-interval presentation, the volume-comparison claim, and the reproducibility gaps must all be fixed, and several of them touch the abstract.

## Reviewer 3 — Talent development and relative-age researcher (registry studies in athletics)

### Summary

The study closes a real gap in the registry literature: it links within-athlete performance trajectories to prospectively defined exit in a complete national athletics population, where previous work followed either rankings (compositional turnover) or participation behaviour, and it reports a pre-specified null for the relative-age-specific mastery prediction without spin. The positioning against the junior-to-senior performance literature is mostly correct, and the explicit "this does not support talent selection" passage (lines 327–328) is exactly right. My main objections concern what the null H2 can and cannot refute — the manuscript tests moderation but the mastery account as originally posed also implies a mediation pathway that the data could test and currently do not — plus an overclaimed novelty statement, the maturation confound, and the rhetorical weight placed on a small penultimate-season decline.

### Major comments

1. **Moderation is tested; mediation is not — and the mastery account needs the latter (lines 76–81, 155–156, 212–217, 284–296).** The companion paper's proposal, as quoted at lines 79–81, is that visible personal progress retains athletes "particularly those born late in the selection year". The manuscript operationalises this solely as effect-measure modification (Δ × birth quarter), finds a null, and concludes against "the relative-age-specific mastery prediction" (lines 42–44, 292–293, 356–359). But there is a second, equally natural reading: because the Tyrving tables are calibrated to calendar-year age classes (lines 102–103, 118–123), relatively younger (Q4) athletes carry a within-class maturity deficit at 13–14 that closes with age, so they should *experience more measured progression* on average. The mastery mechanism would then operate through exposure (late-born athletes stay because they progress more), i.e. mediation of the companion study's Q-trend in retention (OR 1.16 per quarter, line 294), with no interaction required. Two cheap analyses would settle this: (a) report the distribution of Δ by birth quarter (does Q4 progress more?); (b) re-estimate the birth-quarter trend on retention with and without Δ in the model and report the attenuation. If the trend attenuates, the mastery account survives in mediated form and Section 4.3 must be rewritten; if it does not, the manuscript's conclusion gains real strength. Either way the current inference from a null interaction alone overreaches.

2. **The novelty claim is too absolute (lines 81–83).** "No population study has linked objective within-athlete performance trajectories to subsequent dropout, in this or, to our knowledge, any comparable sport system" is a strong priority claim in a literature that includes large registry and career-trajectory analyses in swimming and athletics relating performance development to continued participation and junior-to-senior transition. The discussion would benefit from engaging the longitudinal performance-trajectory literature in individual CGS sports (career progression modelling, early-success-and-persistence analyses) and from softening the claim to what is defensible, e.g. "to our knowledge, no study with complete national coverage has tested within-athlete progression against a prospectively defined exit outcome while adjusting for the behavioural signal". The second "to our knowledge" belongs in the first half of the sentence too.

3. **Maturation should be treated as the leading rival explanation, not the third item in a list (lines 300–303, 340–342; abstract lines 44–46).** In the 13–16 window, biological maturation plausibly drives both measured age-graded progression (calendar-age-calibrated tables cannot distinguish a late maturer from a stagnating athlete) and exit, and it is correlated with birth quarter within age class — so it is entangled with both H1 and H2. The limitation is acknowledged at lines 340–342, but the abstract's closing triad ("commitment, injury, and development", line 46) and the Conclusions ("commitment, injury, or motivation", lines 360–361) never name maturation explicitly, and the two triads differ from each other. Name maturation in both, align the lists, and add two or three sentences in 4.6 on the direction of the bias (late maturers stagnating on calendar-age tables and exiting would mimic the mastery pattern without any motivational mechanism).

4. **"Retention" mixes elite continuation and recreational persistence, and the paper should show whether the signal depends on that (lines 134–138, 327–328).** Two results in a calendar year at age ≥20 is a sensible participation outcome, but for this journal's readership the natural follow-up is whether progression predicts remaining active *at a meaningful performance level* or merely remaining in the registry. A single sensitivity analysis — e.g. the senior-activity outcome with a modest senior points floor, or retention into the national top-N — would either extend the finding or sharpen its participation-only scope. If the authors prefer not to add it, the participation framing should be made explicit in the abstract ("senior activity" is well-defined there, but "retention" in the title and Key Points will be read by talent-development readers as development-relevant).

5. **The "slide before exit" narrative leans on the mechanically contaminated final season (Section 3.5, lines 224–235; Figure 3; abstract lines 35–36; Key Points 54–56).** The authors themselves concede the final-season drop (−47.7) is partly an artefact of fewer attempts behind a mean-of-three-best (lines 231–234) — commendably — but the abstract and Key Points still foreground "7 ... and 48 points" as a pair, and the Conclusions assert stagnation "was visible in the registry a season or more before they left" (lines 355–356) on the strength of a −7.0-point median (CI −14.5 to −1.0) in a selected subsample of 1 011 of 3 160 dropouts. That is a real but modest group-level signal on an ~900-point scale. Please (a) report what fraction of dropouts versus retainers had negative Δ, so the group contrast has an interpretable individual-level counterpart; (b) qualify "48" wherever it appears paired with "7" (e.g. "48, partly mechanical"); and (c) temper "Stagnation, like withdrawal, is visible before the athlete leaves" (lines 234–235) to match the −7-point magnitude.

### Minor comments

- Table 1 reports only Q1 and Q4 shares; give all four quarters (the Q2/Q3 distribution matters for the linear-trend interaction in Table 3).
- Lines 73–76 and 257: report the companion study's volume OR per SD so the "same magnitude" comparison is checkable within this manuscript (see also Reviewer 2's major 3; I agree with it).
- Figure 1: the legend gives group sizes at age 13 only (569/2 340); per-age ns (or a small table) would clarify how much of the dropout curve's late rise is compositional, which the caption correctly explains (lines after Figure 1).
- Lines 346–348: the generalisability paragraph is good; one sentence noting that Norwegian children's-sport regulations preclude early selection (and that the registry starts analysis at 13 for that reason, lines 175–176) would help readers from gate-kept systems calibrate.
- References [10] (under review) and [12] (in press) carry much of the motivation and the tested prediction; please supply both companion manuscripts to the reviewers/editors with the revision, since the present paper cannot be evaluated fully without them.
- Line 275 "Current form, not early rank, is what the retention signal tracks" is a quotable and accurate summary — consider promoting it to the abstract in place of the problematic volume comparison.

### Recommendation

**Major revision.** The core contribution is real and well executed, but the mechanism conclusion needs the mediation analysis (or substantial retreat), the novelty and magnitude claims need tightening, and maturation needs honest top-billing among rival explanations.

## Editor synthesis

**Convergent points (must be addressed).**

1. **The mechanism conclusion overreaches the design.** R1 (majors 1–2) and R3 (major 1) converge from different directions: the null Δ × birth-quarter interaction refutes only one narrow formulation of the mastery account. R3's mediation analysis (progression distributions by quarter; attenuation of the Q-trend when Δ is added) and R1's rank-based moderator are concrete, feasible-in-the-data analyses; at least the mediation analysis should be added, and the "mastery prediction failed" language in abstract, Key Points, 4.3 and Conclusions softened to match what is tested.
2. **The "as strong as competition volume" headline conflicts with Table 2.** R2 (major 3) documents it; R3 (minor) independently asks for the companion coefficient. This claim appears in the abstract, Key Points, 4.1 and Conclusions and must be corrected or precisely sourced.
3. **Numeric and reporting hygiene.** R2's flow-count error (3 808 − 43 ≠ 3 781) must be fixed; STROBE flow diagram and checklist supplied; E-value conversion stated; CV "95 % intervals" relabelled or replaced with proper CIs (this also affects the strength of the H3 conclusion).
4. **Individual-level calibration of the practical message.** R1 (major 4) and R3 (major 5) agree that the −7-point penultimate-season signal and the per-SD odds ratio are group-level findings presented, in places, with individual-level rhetoric; SDs in points and a dropout-vs-retainer share with negative Δ would ground this.

**Points of disagreement or difference in emphasis.**

- R1 wants the discussion anchored in motivational theory (self-efficacy, perceived competence, achievement goals), whereas R3 presses maturation as the leading rival explanation; these are compatible but pull the discussion in different directions, and the authors should expand both rather than pick one.
- R2 regards the design, pre-commitment and robustness programme as exemplary and the issues as presentational; R3 considers the mechanism inference structurally incomplete without the mediation analysis. The revision should treat R3's analysis as required, not optional.
- No reviewer questions the central empirical finding (progression predicts senior retention beyond volume, level and result counts), its replication across cohorts, or the honesty of the limitations section.

**Overall recommendation: Major revision.** All three reviewers see a publishable core — a complete-population test of a pre-specified question, transparently reported — and all three require substantive changes: two additional analyses that the data support (mediation of the birth-quarter trend; sex and/or rank heterogeneity), correction of a numeric inconsistency and of the volume-magnitude claim, proper uncertainty quantification for the AUC comparisons, STROBE materials, and systematic tempering of mechanism- and practice-level language in the abstract, Key Points and Conclusions.
