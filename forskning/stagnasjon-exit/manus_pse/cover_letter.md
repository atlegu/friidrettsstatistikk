Dear Editors,

Please consider the enclosed manuscript, **"Stagnation before exit: performance progression and retention in youth track and field"**, for publication as an original article in *Psychology of Sport and Exercise*.

**What the study asks.** Mastery-experience accounts of motivation (Bandura, Harter, self-determination theory, achievement goal theory) converge on one prediction: athletes stay where they can observe their own progress, and most strongly where rank-based competence is scarce. Track and field offers an unusually clean test, because every result is an objective self-referenced measure, and the Norwegian Athletics Federation's registry records every sanctioned result nationally. Using 3 877 athletes followed from ages 13–14 to adulthood, the study tests three pre-specified hypotheses: whether within-athlete performance progression predicts senior retention beyond competition volume and performance level (H1), whether the association is stronger among relatively younger athletes, as the reinforcement corollary of the mastery account predicts (H2), and whether progression rather than level carries the performance signal (H3).

**What it finds.** Progression predicted retention (OR 2.24 per SD) independently of volume, level and the number of results, replicated across cohorts and robust to all pre-specified variations; dropouts held their performance until the final season and fell only as their participation collapsed. The relative-age-specific prediction, however, was not supported in either its moderation or its mediated form. The paper therefore reports a theoretically motivated test with a split outcome: a general progression–retention link, and a null on the mechanism-specific prediction, interpreted against four rival processes (motivation, commitment, maturation, injury).

**Why this journal.** The contribution is theoretical as well as empirical: it moves the mastery-experience account from an interpretation offered in the relative-age literature to a direct, pre-specified test with population data, and it reports the result that did not fit. The hypotheses, estimands, model specifications and interpretation rules were committed to version control before any exposure–outcome model was estimated; the study was not externally preregistered, and the manuscript says so.

**Length.** The manuscript runs to about 8 690 words (Word count) including abstract, references, tables and figure legends, somewhat above the 8 000-word guideline. The design combines a landmark cohort, a moderation and a mediation analysis, exit-aligned trajectories and a large set of pre-specified robustness analyses; the robustness table and the deviations from the analysis plan have already been moved to the supplementary material, and the four tables that remain are each cited and non-redundant. I ask the editors to allow the present length.

They examine different exposures and samples; none tests the hypotheses reported here, and there is no overlap in results.
